# A Guide to the Voyage

*This guide is for the players. It tells you about Corrantey, the city that
you sail from, about the* Good Intent*, the ship that you sail on, and about
the voyage west. Read it before the first session. Everything here is what
a sailor on the docks of Corrantey knows, or thinks they know, on the
morning that the* Good Intent *leaves the harbor.*

## The Last Morning in Port

> The tide turns an hour before dawn. The *Good Intent* is the only ship in
> the harbor of Corrantey with her sails loose, and half the docks have come
> down in the dark to watch her go.
>
> Some of them came to wave. Most of them came to tell you that you are
> mad. A fishwife on the Corrin quay shouts it as the lines come off. A boy
> on the harbor wall shouts it too, because she did. An old sailor with no
> teeth does not shout anything. He takes off his cap and holds it to his
> chest, as people do at a funeral, and he does not put it back on until
> you pass the harbor light.
>
> Then the city is behind you, the sun comes up over it, and ahead of you
> there is only the Western Sea, grey and cold and wide. Nobody aboard has
> ever seen the far side of it. Nobody alive has.

## Corrantey

Corrantey is the largest city of the land, with about fifteen thousand
people. It stands on the west coast, where two short rivers, the Corrin and
the Tey, reach the sea. Everything that comes in or out of the west of the
land passes through its harbor: wool, salt, wine, oil, fish, and news.

### Two towns, one city

Corrantey was once two towns, Corrin and Tey, about a mile apart. They
were rivals in everything for four hundred years. Each was sure that the
merchants of the other were thieves. Then, about ninety years ago, the sea
silted up the harbor of Corrin and a storm broke the harbor wall of Tey, in
the same autumn. The two towns had one choice: work together or starve
apart. The guilds of both towns signed a charter, built one great harbor
between the two river mouths, and grew into one city.

The city still argues with itself. The old towns are still there, as two
halves of the city:

- **The Corrin half** has the docks, the shipyards, the rope walks, and the
  warehouses. It smells of tar and fish. Its people say that the Tey half
  does nothing but count.
- **The Tey half** has the guild halls, the counting houses, and the money.
  It smells of ink and candle smoke. Its people say that the Corrin half
  cannot count.

The *Good Intent* was built in the Corrin yards and paid for with Tey money.
Everybody in the city has an opinion about that.

### Who rules

Corrantey has no lord and no king. A council of twelve guild masters rules
it, one seat for each guild, at a round table so that nobody sits at the
head. The council meets in the Council House, which stands on the bridge
between the two halves, so that neither half can say that it owns it. The
council argues about everything. It is a free city of merchants, and in
Corrantey everything is for sale.

### Places in the city

- **The Harbor.** The great harbor between the two river mouths, with the
  harbor light on its wall. Ships from up and down the coast tie up here.
  The fish market on the quay smells as it always did.
- **The Council House.** On the bridge between the two halves. People go
  there to watch the council argue. It is free, and it is better than most
  plays.
- **Lantern Street.** The street of the Lampwrights, the guild of lantern
  makers, in the Tey half. Every shop sells light: lanterns, lamps,
  candles, oil, wicks, and glass. At night it is the brightest street in
  the land. The Hall of the Lampwrights stands at the top of it. The guild
  is the oldest in the city, and it never lets you forget it.
- **The theaters.** Corrantey loves plays. There are three companies in the
  city, and each one thinks that the other two cannot act. Sailors go to the
  plays on their last night ashore, for luck.

### Life in Corrantey

- **Money.** Corrantey stamps a lamp on its gold, silver, and copper
  pieces. Coins of other places are good too. Use the normal prices of the
  Player's Compendium.
- **Speech.** People in the city talk fast and drop the ends of their words.
  They greet each other with "Wake well", and the answer is "And see the
  morning." When something goes wrong, they say "Darkit."
- **Sailors.** A sailor of Corrantey is from the Corrin half, whatever the
  sailor says, and has a cousin on every ship in the harbor.

## The Western Sea

The Western Sea is grey, cold, and rich in fish. Ships of Corrantey go up
and down the coast with cargo and news. They do not go far out. Nobody
knows what is out there, and nobody has ever come back to say.

The sailors of Corrantey say that a ship that loses sight of land is a ship
that loses its way. They steer by the headlands and the church towers of
the coast, and they come home before dark when they can. The charts of the
city show the coast in fine detail, every rock and every sandbar. West of
the coast, the charts are empty.

### The lost fleet

Every child in Corrantey knows the story of the lost fleet.

Long ago, before the old kingdom fell, Corrin and Tey were still two towns
that hated each other. Once, only once, they worked together. They built a
fleet of eleven ships and filled it with about seven hundred people:
sailors, farmers, carpenters, priests, and whole families with their
children and their animals. The fleet sailed west to find land beyond the
sea and to settle it. The flagship was the *Morning Star*, of Corrin.

The fleet never came back. Not one ship, not one sailor, not one plank on
the beach.

Each town said that the other town lost it. Tey said that the captains of
Corrin did not know the sea. Corrin said that the ships of Tey were rotten.
They said it for centuries, until they became one city, and the
old families still say it at weddings. Many families of Corrantey have a
name on the list of the lost: a great-great-grandmother who sailed, or a
brother who stayed behind on the quay and watched.

That was three hundred and sixty years ago. The fleet is a story now. The
sailors sing about it.

> *Eleven ships went out from the Corrin quay,*
> *and the Morning Star was the first,*
> *and the wives of Tey said "a fair wind to you,"*
> *and the wives of Corrin cursed.*
>
> *Eleven ships went out at the turn of the tide,*
> *and the Morning Star led them on.*
> *Now who will go and look for them?*
> *Not I, my love. Not your son.*

## The *Good Intent*

The *Good Intent* is a merchant ship of Corrantey: broad in the beam, deep
in the hold, and slow, but she does not mind a heavy sea. She has two
masts, a crew of about twenty, and room for a few passengers aft. She
carries one ship's boat on her deck. Her figurehead is a woman who holds up
a lamp, the mark of the city. The sailors call her "the Lady", and they
touch her for luck when they go aboard.

She is not new. She has carried wool and salt up and down the coast for
twenty years, and every sailor in the Corrin half has worked on her at
some time. Her owners had her new-coppered, new-rigged, and new-painted for
this voyage. She looks very handsome. She also looks very small, next to
the Western Sea.

### The people aboard

The GM gives you the names of the people aboard in the first session.

| Who | What you know |
|---|---|
| The master | The captain of the ship. Twenty years on the coast and never lost a ship. Nobody else wanted to take this voyage. |
| The mate | The second in command, who runs the deck and the crew. Loud, fair, and always awake. |
| The bosun | The keeper of the sails, the ropes, and the boat. Knows every knot that was ever tied. |
| The cook | The most important person aboard, the crew says. Also the ship's surgeon, in an emergency. |
| The crew | About fifteen sailors of the Corrin half, and two boys and a girl who are learning the sea. |
| The passengers | You, and anybody else whom your group decides is aboard. |

### The articles

Each person who sails on the *Good Intent* signs her articles, the contract
of the voyage. The master reads them aloud on the quay before anybody
signs.

> *Articles of the ship* Good Intent *of Corrantey, for a voyage west.*
>
> *One. Each person who signs these articles sails west with the ship, as
> far as the master judges good, and comes home with her.*
>
> *Two. The owners give food, water, and a dry place to sleep. Each person
> brings the rest.*
>
> *Three. Whatever the voyage finds, land or fish or trade, the owners take
> half. The other half is shared among all who signed, one share each, and
> two for the master.*
>
> *Four. Each person obeys the master at sea. On land, each person obeys
> the master in what touches the ship.*
>
> *Five. Each person who signs comes home with the ship, or the ship does
> not come home.*

The last article is old. Every ship of Corrantey has it. Nobody remembers
who wrote it.

## The Voyage West

### Why she sails

For three hundred and sixty years, nobody sailed west on purpose. So why
now?

The answer depends on who you ask on the docks:

- **The owners**, a group of merchants of the Tey half, say that there is
  land out there, and that the first ship to find it will make the city
  rich: new land to farm, new banks to fish, new people to trade with.
- **The fishermen** say that the fish moved west. The catch on the coast is
  thinner each year, and the boats must go farther out each spring.
- **The old sailors** say that the lost fleet must have found something,
  because seven hundred people do not just disappear.
- **The theaters** already have a play about it. In the play, the *Good
  Intent* finds an island of gold and a queen who falls in love with the
  master. The master did not go to see it.

### The plan

The *Good Intent* sails west from Corrantey on the turn of the tide, at the
start of summer, when the sea is kindest. She carries food and water for
three months. The plan is simple: sail west for one month, and look. If
she finds land, she marks it on the chart, takes what she can, and comes
home to tell the city. If she finds nothing in one month, she turns back.

The master keeps a log each day. The mate takes the depth of the water each
noon. A passenger with a good hand draws the chart. Each evening, the whole
ship looks at the empty part of the chart, and wonders.

### Life aboard

- **The watches.** The crew works in two watches, four hours on and four
  hours off, day and night. A passenger who wants to help is welcome. A
  passenger who is in the way is not.
- **The food.** Salt fish, salt pork, hard biscuit, dried peas, and beer.
  Fresh bread for the first three days. After that, the cook does what the
  cook can.
- **Space.** There is very little. Each passenger has a sea chest and a
  hammock or a narrow bunk. Heavy armor goes in the hold, wrapped in oiled
  cloth, or it rusts.
- **Luck.** Sailors whistle for a wind and never on a calm day. They touch
  the Lady before they go aboard. They never say the word "drown" at sea.
  They will be very upset if you do.

## Why You Are Aboard

Each character has a reason to be on the *Good Intent*. Choose one from the
table, or roll a d6. You can also make up your own with the GM.

| d6 | Why you are aboard |
|---|---|
| 1 | **The lost name.** Your family has a name on the list of the lost fleet. You grew up on the story. You want to know what happened to them. |
| 2 | **The crew.** You signed the articles as a sailor, a ship's carpenter, a cook's help, or a ship's boy or girl. The sea is your trade, and the share of a voyage like this could make you rich. |
| 3 | **The owners' eyes.** The merchants who paid for the voyage sent you to watch their money: the cargo, the log, and the master. The master knows why you are aboard and does not like it. |
| 4 | **The scholar.** You study old charts, old ships, or old stories. You paid for your passage to see the far side of the sea with your own eyes, and to write the book about it. |
| 5 | **The guard.** The owners hired you to protect the ship and to go ashore first, if there is a shore. You are paid by the month, and you are paid to be brave. |
| 6 | **Away from the city.** You needed to leave Corrantey quickly: a debt, a feud, a broken promise, or a mistake. Nobody will look for you in the middle of the Western Sea. |

Then choose one other character aboard whom you knew before the voyage.
Roll a d6 or choose how you know them.

| d6 | How you know them |
|---|---|
| 1 | You grew up on the same street, in the same half of the city. |
| 2 | You owe them money, or they owe you. Neither of you has said it aloud. |
| 3 | You sailed together before, on a short and miserable voyage up the coast. |
| 4 | You are family: cousins, or something more complicated. |
| 5 | You met in a tavern on the night before the voyage, and you told them a secret. |
| 6 | You were on opposite sides of an argument in the city, and you both signed the articles to prove a point. |

### What you bring

- **Your gear.** Make your character with the Player's Compendium, with the
  starting equipment of your class and background. Everything that you
  carry fits in your sea chest or on your back.
- **Your share.** Each character who signed the articles has one share of
  whatever the voyage finds. A share is worth nothing today. It could be
  worth a fortune tomorrow.
- **One keepsake.** Each character brings one small thing from home that
  matters: a letter, a coin, a carving, a lock of hair. Tell the GM what it
  is.

## The Players' Cheat Sheet: Six-Sided Dice

*Use this page if your group plays with the dice of a backgammon set: four
six-sided dice and a doubling cube. Make your character with normal dice,
as the Player's Compendium says. Change the dice at the table with this
page. The rolls in the GM's book already show the six-sided form, in
brackets.*

### The roll of the twenty-sided die

For an **attack roll**, an **ability check**, or a **saving throw**, roll
**3d6**. Add the same modifiers as always. The DC and the Armor Class do
not change.

| You have | Roll |
|---|---|
| A normal roll | 3d6 |
| Advantage | 4d6, keep the 3 highest |
| Disadvantage | 4d6, keep the 3 lowest |

| Rule | With 3d6 |
|---|---|
| A critical hit, or a natural 20 | A natural 16, 17, or 18 |
| A natural 1 | A natural 3, 4, or 5 |
| A death save | 10 or more succeeds. A natural 16 or more counts as a natural 20. A natural 5 or less counts as a natural 1. |

### The other dice

| Die | Roll | Average |
|---|---|---|
| d4 | d6 minus 1, but a 1 stays 1 | about 2.5 |
| d6 | d6 | 3.5 |
| d8 | d6 + 1 | 4.5 |
| d10 | d6 + 2 | 5.5 |
| d12 | d6 + 3 | 6.5 |

- **Change each die.** Two d8s are 2d6 + 2. A d10 and a d4 are 2d6 + 1.
- **Hit Dice change too.** A d8 Hit Die is d6 + 1.
- **A critical hit:** double the dice first, then change them. A d8 on a
  critical hit is 2d6 + 2.
- **More than four dice:** roll the four dice, pick them up, roll again,
  and add.

### Your character sheet

Keep the normal dice on your sheet, and write the six-sided form next to
each roll. For example:

| On your sheet | Write next to it |
|---|---|
| Longsword: 1d8 + 3 slashing | d6 + 4 |
| Greataxe: 1d12 + 3 slashing | d6 + 6 |
| Dagger: 1d4 + 2 piercing | d6 + 1, minimum 3 |
| Cure Wounds: 2d8 + 3 | 2d6 + 5 |

### The cube

The doubling cube sits on the table. It starts on 2, and the GM turns it
up when time runs short. Once in each session, you can **double**: turn it
up one face yourself, for a big benefit such as a reroll or a quick rest.
The table can double only once in each scene. Each double brings the cube
nearer to 64.
