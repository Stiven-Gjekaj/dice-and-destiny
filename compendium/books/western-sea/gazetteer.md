# Gazetteer of Farholm

This gazetteer is for the GM. It tells the story of the lost fleet, shows
the colony of Hythe and its two peoples, walks the island from shore to
shore, and, at the end, tells the truth about the Grandam and the Wall.
The players learn it at the table, one conversation and one cliff path at
a time.

Use it in three ways:

- **Before the first session.** Read "The Lost Fleet", "Hythe", and "The
  Corrin Men and the Tey Folk". They tell you who the party meets on the
  shore and what each person wants from them.
- **When the party goes somewhere.** Each place of the island has a short
  entry. The chapters give the full sites. The entries here tell you what
  the place is like on an ordinary day, and who goes there.
- **When you prepare the last chapter.** Read "The Truth of the Grandam".
  It is the secret that the whole book turns on.

### Secrets

Some facts here are for a later chapter. A box with the label **Secret**
marks each one, with the chapter where the players can learn it:

> **Secret (chapter 3):** An example. Do not tell this before chapter 3.

Most colonists do not know the secrets. When a person knows one, or half
of one, the entry says so.

### What the colony does not know

The fleet left old Ostrey sixty years before the long sleep of the
Dawnwatch. The colonists know the land as it was then, and nothing since.
They have never heard of the Unlit King, the Grey Winter, the fall of
Ostrey, Gaunt, the Church of the Five Sleepers, or Corrantey. To them,
Corrin and Tey are two proud towns a mile apart that hate each other, and
King Edwy sits on the throne in Kingsholm.

The party knows better. Corrin and Tey grew into one city, Corrantey,
about ninety years ago, and few people there remember the fleet as more
than an old sailors' tale. Each time the party talks about home, it can
break a colonist's heart or start a fight. Let the players find this out
slowly. It is one of the best things in the book.

## The Lost Fleet

The colonists count the years from the Landing, the day the fleet came
ashore. The Landing is their year 0. Our year 300 is "the year 360 of the
Landing". The years in this section use their count first and ours in
brackets.

### The sailing

Corrin and Tey were rivals in everything: fish, trade, saints, and
songs. In the spring of year 0 of the Landing, an old pilot of Tey told
both towns that land lay beyond the Western Sea, because the birds flew
that way each autumn and came back fat. Neither town could pay for a
fleet alone, and neither would let the other go first. So, for the only
time in their history, they sailed together.

Six ships came from Corrin: the *Morning Star*, the *Speedwell*, the
*Hopewell*, the *Swallow*, the *Increase*, and the *Desire*. Five came
from Tey: the *Patience*, the *Dove*, the *Blessing*, the *Content*, and
the *Unity*. They carried about seven hundred people, with sheep, seed,
tools, and the bells of two chapels. Captain Humphrey Gale of Corrin,
master of the *Morning Star*, led the fleet. Magnus Isbister of Tey,
master of the *Patience*, led the ships of Tey, and he never let anyone
forget that he did not take orders from Corrin.

### The Landing

Forty days out, the sky to the west went black from end to end. The
records of the colony call it "a wall of weather, standing on the sea
like a cliff". Captain Gale tried to turn the fleet. The wind would not
let him. It blew toward the Wall from every side, and it took the ships
in.

Nobody who lived wrote much about the night inside the Wall. In the
morning, the sea "stood up" under them. The ships broke on the reefs of
the south and north coasts. The *Morning Star* went highest of all: the
sea set her down on the stony ridges of the Ribs, miles from the shore,
and she is still there.

About seven hundred people came ashore. About five hundred lived through
the first winter.

### Saint Mawes and the first winter

The first winter was hungry and cold. Father Mawes, a priest of the
Morning Flame from the *Patience*, walked out to a bare hollow in the west
of the island to light the dawn fire for the feast of midwinter. There
was no stone there, so he lit it on the ground.

The ground shook. The colonists in Hythe felt the island lean, and the
sea ran out of the bay and back. Father Mawes put out his fire with his
own cloak and knelt on the ash and sang the dawn hymn until the ground
was still. He died three years later of a fever, and the colony, with no
bishop to tell it no, named him a saint. The hollow where he lit his fire
is the Saint's Hearth. The church tells the story every midwinter. See
"The Church of the Morning Flame".

### The Corrin Light

In the first ten years, Captain Gale built a tower of stone on the east
point of the island, the point nearest home. He put the great lamp of the
*Morning Star* at the top: a lamp of brass with a bright mirror behind it
and a thick lens of glass in front of it. He said that a light on the
east point would bring the ships of Corrin to find them. No ship came.
The Corrin men lit the lamp each night for almost two hundred years.

Captain Gale died in the year 19 of the Landing. By then, Hythe had split
along the stream called the Fleet: the people of the Corrin ships on the
east bank, the people of the Tey ships on the west. The old rivalry came
ashore with them, and it never left.

### The *Hopewell* year and the Drowning

In the year 211 of the Landing (year 151), the young men of the Corrin
bank built a new ship from the timbers of the old wrecks. They gave it
the name of one of the lost ships of Corrin, the *Hopewell*. John Pascoe
was its master.

They waited for the storm season, when the Wall comes in over the island,
because John Pascoe had read in Captain Gale's notes that "the Wall is
thinnest when it is nearest". On the worst night of the Turning, they
lit the Corrin Light with all the oil in Hythe. And the Wall opened: a
lane of quiet water, straight out to the east, with stars over it. The
*Hopewell* sailed into it.

On the Tey bank, people felt the ground move, as it moved under the
saint. Sander Linklater led a crowd of Tey men up to the east point. They
broke into the tower, put out the lamp, and smashed its lens. The lane of
quiet water closed. The storm raged on for nine more days. When it
ended, the Boneyard on the north coast held one more heap of broken
timber among a hundred older ones. The Tey folk said that it was the
*Hopewell*, thrown back by the Grandam. The Corrin men searched the
beach, found nothing with a name on it, and said that the Tey folk were
lying to hide what they had done. The *Hopewell* never came home, and
nobody on her was seen again.

In the same storm season, the sea came up the Fleet in the night and
drowned the low part of Hythe, on the Tey bank. Forty people died. The
colonists call it the Drowning.

### The Long Quiet

The next year, the Moot of Hythe forbade anyone to light the Corrin
Light. The Corrin men agreed, because there was no lens and almost no
lamp left to light. The Tey folk carried the pieces of the lamp away.
Some went down the Grandam's Well as gifts. Some families kept a piece
over the hearth. The Corrin men saved the brass frame, and they keep a
watch at the ruin to this day.

That was nearly a hundred and fifty years ago. The colony has lived in an
uneasy peace since: one town, two banks, one bridge, and one church with
two sides to its aisle.

> **Secret (chapter 2):** The *Hopewell* did not get through. The lane
> closed when the light went out, and the storms threw her back. Her name
> board lies in the Boneyard under the timbers of older wrecks. The Corrin
> men believe that she reached home, and that Corrin will send ships one
> day. The Tey folk believe that the Grandam threw her back in anger. The
> name board proves the first belief wrong and does not prove the second.

## Hythe

*The colony town at the mouth of the Fleet. About 400 people. Chapters 1,
2, and 3.*

Hythe is an old word for a landing place, and that is all the town is:
the place where the colonists landed and stayed. It sits where the Fleet,
a quick brown stream, runs out of the hills into a small sheltered bay on
the south-east coast. From the path over the Saltpans, the party sees it
all at once: a jumble of grey roofs on two slopes, a stone bridge between
them, smoke from a hundred chimneys, a church on the green hill behind,
and boats pulled up on the shingle.

Up close, everything is old and mended. The houses are built of wreck
timber, black with tar, with walls of turf and roofs of turf that grow
grass and daisies. Some houses are the hulls of ships' boats, turned
upside down on low stone walls, with a door cut in the stern. Each window
is small, because glass ran out two hundred years ago, and most are
covered with scraped sealskin. The streets are paths of shingle and
whale bone, and nothing on wheels is bigger than a barrow.

There is no town map in this book. Use the description here, and let the
players draw it if they like. Four things matter: the two banks, the
Bridge between them, the halls where each side meets, and the church on
the hill.

### The Corrin bank

The east bank of the Fleet belongs to the Corrin men. About 180 people
live there, under the hill that leads toward the Corrin Light.

- **The Lamp Room.** The hall of the Corrin men, a long low house with
  the figurehead of the *Desire* over its door. A lamp of seal oil burns
  in its east window every night, toward home. The Corrin men meet here
  to drink, to argue, and to read aloud from Captain Gale's notes. The
  brass frame of the great lamp of the Light hangs on its wall, black
  with age.
- **The slip.** A shingle ramp at the mouth of the Fleet with three boat
  sheds. The boats of the Corrin men are fishing boats. None goes out of
  sight of land, because the Wall is twenty miles out and everyone knows
  what it does. The last shed is locked. See Margery Nance.
- **The school.** One room over the sail loft, where Nathaniel Gale
  teaches the children of both banks their letters and their sums, in
  suns, stars, and bits. Most Tey children stopped coming two years ago.

### The Tey bank

The west bank belongs to the Tey folk. About 220 people live there, more
than on the Corrin bank, but in fewer and fuller houses, high on the
slope. The low part of the bank is empty: the sea took it in the
Drowning. Grass grows over the old foundations, and nobody builds there.
The Tey folk call it the Low Ground, and children are told not to play
on it.

- **The Moot House.** The great cabin of the *Patience*, carried ashore
  whole by the first colonists and set on a stone base near the Bridge.
  Its carved stern windows still have their glass. The Moot meets here.
  The Tey folk are proud that the Moot House stands on their bank, and the
  Corrin men never forget it.
- **The Net Loft.** A big house of turf where the Tey folk mend their nets
  and hold their meetings. The Well Keeper sits by its hearth on most
  evenings, and anyone of the Tey bank can come and speak to her.
- **The well path.** A narrow track runs west out of the Tey bank toward
  the interior. At its start, a sun cross of driftwood stands in a ring of
  white stones. Each Tey person who passes touches it.

### The Bridge

One bridge crosses the Fleet: three low arches of ballast stone, built in
the first ten years by people of both banks together. It is the only
thing in Hythe that both sides call by the same name. The market of Hythe
is on the Bridge on the first day of each week. Fish, salt, eggs, wool,
ale, and work change hands. The Corrin men set up on the east half and
the Tey folk on the west, and the middle arch is for haggling.

A fight on the Bridge is a crime against the Moot. Old people still say
"meet me on the middle arch" when they mean "let us settle this fairly".

### The church

The church of the Morning Flame stands above the town on Founders' Hill.
See "Founders' Hill" in "Places of Farholm" and "The Church of the
Morning Flame".

### The Moot

Hythe has no lord and no mayor. It has the Moot: six headmen from each
bank, who meet in the Moot House at each new moon and on Landing Day. The
priest of the church sits between them and keeps the peace. The Captain
of the Light speaks for the Corrin men. The Well Keeper speaks for the
Tey folk. The Moot decides fishing rights, quarrels, marriages across the
Bridge, and punishments. Its harshest punishment is to send a person to
live outside the town. Its oldest law forbids anyone to light the Corrin
Light.

The Moot is slow and proud, and it has not agreed on anything important
for a hundred years. The arrival of the party is the most important thing
that has happened in Hythe since the Drowning. Both sides want the party
on their bank before the next Moot.

### How Hythe treats strangers

There has never been a stranger in Hythe. The last people from beyond
the Wall were the colonists' own great-great-grandparents. The first
reaction to the party is wonder, then a crowd, then a hundred questions:
"Is it true the king has a palace of glass? Do they still eat white
bread? Which town sent you, Corrin or Tey?"

That last question is the dangerous one. Every person in Hythe wants the
answer to be their own town. See "The Corrin Men and the Tey Folk".

## The Corrin Men and the Tey Folk

The colonists call the two sides "the Corrin men" and "the Tey folk".
The names are old and do not mean what they say: the Corrin men include
women and children, and the Tey folk include grim men with spears. Most
families have married within their own bank for twelve generations.

Neither side is evil, and neither is right. Each has one true thing and
one terrible mistake. Play both with sympathy. The best sessions are the
ones where the players cannot decide whom to help.

### The Corrin men

**What they want.** To go home. Every Corrin child grows up on stories of
Corrin: its harbor, its fish market, its tall houses, its saints' days.
Every Corrin man believes that Corrin is still there, as it was, and
that it waits for them.

**What they believe.** That the Corrin Light can open the Wall, because
it did once. That the *Hopewell* got through and reached home. That
Corrin will send ships one day, and that the Light must be ready when
they come. That the Tey folk are cowards who would rather pray to a hole
in the ground than face the sea.

**What they are right about.** The Light opens a path through the Wall.
John Pascoe saw it, and so did half the Corrin bank.

**What they are wrong about.** The Light does not work on any night. It
works only while the Grandam stirs, which makes it the most dangerous
thing on the island. The *Hopewell* did not get through. And Corrin does
not wait for them. Corrin is half of Corrantey now, a city where nobody
cares whose great-grandfather came from which bank of which river. If
the Corrin men go home, nobody will meet them at the quay.

**What they ask of the party.** Proof that home is real, news of Corrin,
and help to mend the Light before the Turning. They are generous to a
party that brings good news and furious with a party that brings bad
news.

**People.**

- **Absalom Pascoe,** the Captain of the Light. About sixty, square,
  grey, and stubborn, in his grandfather's blue coat. His ancestor John
  Pascoe was master of the *Hopewell*, and he wears the old Pascoe
  cutlass that John left behind for his son. He is honest
  to a fault and has never once doubted that home is there. He will
  accept the party as a gift from Corrin. He will find it very hard to
  believe that Corrin is gone. His daughter married across the Bridge,
  and he has not spoken to her in six years.
- **Kit Hocking,** twenty, red-haired, loud, and brave. He leads the
  young men of the Corrin bank, who are tired of waiting for old men. He
  wants to light the Light now, with or without the Moot, and he thinks
  the party is the sign. He is the most likely person in Hythe to start
  a fight, and the most likely to die in one.
- **Nathaniel Gale,** the schoolmaster, about fifty, thin, short-sighted,
  and gentle. He is the last of the family of Captain Gale, and he keeps
  the Landing Book: the records of the colony, with Captain Gale's own
  notes on the building of the Light and the lamp. He knows more about
  the lamp than anyone alive, and he is afraid of what he knows. He has
  read the notes on the *Hopewell* year more closely than Kit Hocking
  has.
- **Margery Nance,** the shipwright, about forty, broad and calm, with
  tar under her nails. In the locked shed on the slip, she is building a
  boat that can sail through the Wall. Only Absalom and a few others know.
  She builds well, and she will ask the party a great many questions
  about how ships are built today.

> **Secret (chapter 2):** Nathaniel Gale has found a line in the Landing
> Book, in Captain Gale's hand, from the year of the Landing: "The Wall
> drew in when the ground breathed hard, and was quiet where the
> lanterns were." He thinks it means the Light works only in the storm
> season, when the ground moves. He is right, and he has told nobody,
> because it means the only way home is also the most dangerous.

### The Tey folk

**What they want.** To stay, and to keep the Grandam asleep. The Tey
folk do not hate the sea. They fish it every day. But they believe that
Farholm is alive and holy, that it saved them, and that it can kill them
all in a night.

**What they believe.** That the island is the Grandam, a great mother
who carries them on her back and gives them warm springs, fish, and
birds. That fire on her bare back hurts her, as the saint learned. That
lighting the Corrin Light, or trying to leave, wakes her, and that when
she wakes she will sink the island and everyone on it. That the Drowning
was her punishment for the *Hopewell*. That the hymn of the saint keeps
her asleep.

**What they are right about.** The island is alive. Fire on her back
makes her stir. The Light, lit in the storm season, draws her up toward
waking. They have kept, without knowing it, the one thing that can
quiet her: the saint's hymn and the saint's darkness.

**What they are wrong about.** She is not a mother, and she does not
know them. She did not punish anyone. The Drowning came from the storm
season, not the Light. Leaving does not wake her. And this year, she
will stir to waking whether anyone lights the Light or not.

**What they ask of the party.** Respect for their customs, no fire on
the bare ground, and a promise to stay away from the Corrin Light. They
will feed and shelter a party that keeps its promises. They will turn
cold, then dangerous, toward a party that helps the Corrin men.

**People.**

- **Elspeth Flett,** the Well Keeper, about seventy, small, white-haired,
  and very still. She sits by the hearth of the Net Loft and listens more
  than she speaks. She keeps the old dawn hymn of Saint Mawes and leads
  the Tey folk in it at the Well. She is wise, kind to children, and
  utterly certain. She has felt the ground move this year, more than in
  all her life, and she is afraid.
- **Erlend Linklater,** the leader of the Well Watch, about forty, dark,
  quiet, with a scar where his left ear was. The Well Watch are the Tey
  men who keep people away from the Corrin Light, as Erlend's family has
  done since his ancestor Sander Linklater put out the lamp. He does not
  hate the Corrin men. He thinks they will drown his children. He will
  kill to stop them, and he will grieve afterward.
- **Inga Halcrow,** a girl of fourteen, thin, freckled, and much too
  serious. Since the spring, she has dreamed every night of deep water,
  slow cold, and a great weight of sleep. The Tey folk think she is holy,
  and they bring her gifts of eggs and ribbon. She hates it. She would
  very much like someone to tell her she is not mad.
- **Thomas Spence,** a fisherman, about thirty-five, slow, kind, and
  silent. He is the best guide on the island and knows every path, pool,
  and gull cliff. He married Hester Pascoe across the Bridge, and the
  Tey folk forgave him because he is a Spence and a good man. He will
  take the party anywhere except the Corrin Light.

> **Secret (chapter 2):** Inga Halcrow's dreams are the Grandam's dreams.
> Inga is near the Well so often, and sleeps so near the warm ground,
> that the creature's sleep has crept into hers. She can tell the party,
> night by night, how close the Grandam is to waking. When the Storm Cube
> turns up, Inga's dreams change: from cold and dark, to warmth, to light
> far above, to a great slow rising.

### Between the banks

A few people belong to neither side, or to both.

- **Hester Spence,** born Hester Pascoe, about thirty, the daughter of
  Absalom Pascoe and the wife of Thomas Spence. She lives on the Tey bank
  with two small children who have never met their grandfather. She is
  quick, warm, and tired of both sides. Neither side fully trusts her,
  and she is the best person in Hythe for the party to talk to.
- **Father Gilbert Rundle,** the priest. See "The Church of the Morning
  Flame".
- **Amos Tulloch,** an old salt boiler who lives alone at the Saltpans.
  See "The Saltpans".

### The party and the two sides

The chapters use the Factions and renown system of the Game Master's
Compendium for the Corrin men and the Tey folk. Some guidance for the
whole book:

- **Every act is seen.** Hythe is small. What the party does on one bank
  is known on the other by supper.
- **Neither side is a villain.** Even Erlend Linklater and Kit Hocking
  act from love: of children, of home. Let a player who sides with one
  of them see the other side's grief.
- **The question of home.** When a Corrin man asks about Corrin, the
  truth is painful. When a Tey person asks, the truth is a relief: there
  is nothing to go home to, so why risk the Grandam? Either way, the
  party's news changes the balance in Hythe.
- **The middle arch.** The best ending for Hythe needs both sides. Give
  the party chances to bring them together: Hester and her father, Inga
  and Nathaniel Gale, Margery Nance and Thomas Spence.

## The Church of the Morning Flame

The colonists pray to the Morning Flame, the god of the dawn, as old
Ostrey did. The faith on Farholm is the faith of 360 years ago, kept
carefully and changed a little by the island.

### The church on the hill

The church stands at the top of Founders' Hill, above Hythe: a long,
low, stone building with a bell tower made from the mainmast of the
*Speedwell*. Its two bells came from the chapels of Corrin and Tey, and
the colonists say that they ring a little out of tune with each other,
which is true. The graves of the first colonists lie around it.

Inside, the Corrin men sit on the left of the aisle and the Tey folk on
the right. Nobody made a rule. Nobody breaks it. A faded red banner of
the Morning Flame hangs over the altar.

The priest lights the dawn fire every morning, as in old Ostrey, but on
Farholm it burns in an iron dish on a ballast stone, never on the floor.
The whole colony knows why.

### Father Gilbert Rundle

The priest is Father Gilbert Rundle, about sixty-five, round, red-faced,
and fond of ale and arguments. He wears the red and gold of the Morning
Flame, patched and faded. He was born on the Corrin bank and has spent
forty years making sure that nobody can tell. He keeps the peace at the
Moot, preaches the legend of the saint every midwinter, and buries the
dead of both banks in the same ground.

He is a good man and a tired one. He believes that the colony will tear
itself apart one day, and he is afraid that the party is the day. He
will help the party if they promise him, sincerely, that they will try
to keep both sides alive.

### The legend of Saint Mawes

Every year, on the night of Saint Mawes's Day, at midwinter, Father
Rundle tells the legend in the church. This is how he tells it:

> In the first winter, when the Landing was new and the people were
> cold and hungry, Father Mawes of the *Patience* went out alone at
> night, to the west, to find a high place to greet the dawn. He found a
> hollow in the turf where no snow lay, and there he made the dawn fire,
> because the Morning Flame must be greeted, even at the end of the
> world.
>
> And the ground moved under him. The whole land leaned, like a ship in
> a swell, and in Hythe the sea ran out of the bay and came roaring back.
> And Father Mawes understood that the land was not a land, and that he
> had lit a fire on something that lived.
>
> So he put out the fire with his own cloak, and burned his hands to do
> it. And he knelt on the ash in the dark, and sang the dawn hymn softly,
> as a mother sings to a child, until the land was still. And when the
> dawn came, he walked home and told the people: "We are guests here.
> Light your fires on the stones you brought, and never on the ground."
>
> And so we do. Light to you.

And the people answer: "And dawn to you."

### Three tellings

Everyone in Hythe knows the legend, and each side hears something
different in it.

- **The church** teaches that the saint was humble and wise, and that
  the lesson is to live gently on the island. Father Rundle stops there,
  on purpose.
- **The Tey folk** hear that the land is a living thing, a mother who
  must be sung to sleep, and that fire wakes her. The worship of the
  Grandam grew from this story. The Tey folk keep the saint's hymn and
  sing it at the Grandam's Well each week. The Well Keeper says that the
  hymn keeps her sleeping.
- **The Corrin men** hear that the land moved when a fire was lit, and
  that the sea ran out and came back. Some of them, Kit Hocking first,
  wonder if a big enough fire could move the land on purpose, or open a
  way through the sea. John Pascoe wondered the same, 150 years ago.

> **Secret (chapter 3):** The legend is true, almost word for word. The
> saint's fire made the Grandam stir. His darkness and his hymn let her
> settle again. The hymn has no magic in it. What matters is what the
> saint did: he put out the fire, went down close to her in the dark, and
> was quiet. The last chapter uses this, if the party wants the Grandam
> to sleep again.

### The Grandam in the church

Father Rundle does not preach the Grandam. He does not forbid her, either.
The Tey folk pray to the Morning Flame in the church and to the Grandam
at the Well, and they see no conflict. Father Rundle calls the Grandam
"the island" in his sermons, and both sides hear what they want.

## Places of Farholm

Farholm is about 20 miles from west to east and 12 miles from north to
south. Hythe is on the south-east coast. A walker can reach any place on
the island from Hythe in a day, so the Journeys system on Farholm counts
hours, not days. The chapters give the rules.

The island has no roads, only tracks and paths. A track is wide enough
for a barrow. A path is for feet only. Off the paths, the ground is turf,
bog, and bare stone: Difficult Terrain in many places.

## The South Coast

The coast where the *Good Intent* wrecks, and where the party first sets
foot on Farholm.

### Wreckers' Bay

*A wide bay of grey sand on the south coast. Chapter 1.*

A long curve of grey sand, three miles from end to end, with a reef
across its mouth that shows white at low tide. Every ship that the Wall
ever took in came ashore here or on the north coast. The colonists call
it Wreckers' Bay, though they never wreck anything: the sea does it for
them. After every storm, people from Hythe walk over the Saltpans to see
what the sea has brought. Usually it is timber, weed, and dead fish.

The sand is full of old things: rusted nails, green coins, a bell with no
clapper, the bones of ships' boats. A party that searches finds
something old in every hour.

**What the party finds.** The sea, the shore, the first look at the
island, and the first colonists. Chapter 1 gives the full scenes.

### The wreck of the Good Intent

*The merchant ship of the party, broken on the reef off Wreckers' Bay.
Chapter 1.*

The *Good Intent* sits on the reef a quarter of a mile out, broken
behind the mainmast, her stern under water at high tide. At low tide a
strong swimmer can reach her, and a careful one can climb aboard. The
cargo, the stores, and the tools that the party saves from her are the
richest things on Farholm. To the colonists, a barrel of flour or a box
of iron nails is a treasure.

The sea takes a little more of her each day. After a few days, the
stern goes. After the first storm, she breaks up.

### The Saltpans

*Shallow pans on the shore between Wreckers' Bay and Hythe. Chapters 1
and 2.*

A flat stretch of shore, a mile long, cut into shallow square pans with
low walls of clay. The sea fills them at high tide, and the sun and the
wind dry them. At the east end, a stone hut with a big iron pan over a
peat fire boils the brine down to salt. Salt is the money of Farholm, and
the Saltpans make all of it. The path from Wreckers' Bay to Hythe runs
along the pan walls.

**People.**

- **Amos Tulloch,** the salt boiler, about seventy-five, bent, brown as
  leather, and cheerful. He was born on the Tey bank. He married a Corrin
  girl fifty years ago, and the Moot never forgave either of them, so
  they came out here to boil salt. His wife died ten years ago. He talks
  to her still, out loud, and he is not mad. He belongs to neither side,
  likes everybody, and believes nothing that he has not seen. He is the
  first colonist the party meets, and the best one to explain Hythe
  without taking a side.

> **GM tip:** Amos is a good voice for the GM. When the players are lost
> among names, let them walk back to the Saltpans and ask him. He knows
> everyone, he has an opinion of everyone, and his opinions are fair.

## Hythe and the East

The town, the hill above it, and the point that looks toward home.

### Hythe

See "Hythe", above.

### The Fleet

*The stream from the Kettles to the sea at Hythe. Chapters 1 and 2.*

A quick brown stream, warm at its head and cold at its mouth, that runs
about eight miles from the Kettles to Hythe. It splits the town into the
Corrin bank and the Tey bank. The colonists named it after the fleet,
because, they say, it brought them home to Hythe the way the fleet
brought them to Farholm. In the Drowning, the sea came up it at night.
The track from Hythe to the Kettles follows its valley.

### Founders' Hill

*A green hill above Hythe, with the church and the graves of the first
colonists. Chapter 2.*

A round green hill just north-west of the town, grazed short by small brown
sheep. The church of the Morning Flame stands on the top, with the
graveyard around it. The oldest graves have names cut in ship's timber:
"Barbara Rendall of the *Dove*, came ashore 0, died 2". Captain Humphrey Gale lies
in the middle of the graveyard under a slab of the *Morning Star*'s
deck. Saint Mawes lies under the altar.

From the top of the hill, a person can see all of Hythe, the Saltpans,
the east point and the Corrin Light, and, on a clear day, the dark line
of the Wall on every side of the sea.

**What the party finds.** The church, Father Rundle, the names of the
first colonists, and a view of the whole problem at once.

### The Corrin Light

*The broken lighthouse of the fleet, on the east point. Chapters 2 and
3.*

About five miles north-east of Hythe, a bare finger of rock runs out into the
sea toward home. At its end stands the Corrin Light: a round tower of
grey stone, forty feet tall, with a stair inside its wall and a stone
room at the top where the lamp once stood. The roof of the lamp room is
gone, and the gulls nest in it. The door at the bottom is new oak, and it
is locked.

The Corrin men keep a watch here, two men at a time, in a turf hut by the
tower door. They clean the stair, mend the walls, and keep the tower
ready for a lamp that they do not have. Every Corrin child walks here
with a parent once, to look east and be told: "That way is home." The
Tey folk do not come here, except the Well Watch, who keep an eye on the
Corrin watch from the heather on the slope above.

The lamp is gone. The Corrin men keep its brass frame in the Lamp Room in
Hythe. The mirror and the lens were broken in the *Hopewell* year and
carried away by the Tey folk. To light the Corrin Light again, the party
must find or make a lamp, a mirror, a lens, and enough oil, and carry
them up the tower. The chapters say where the pieces are.

> **Secret (chapter 2):** The Light opens a path through the Wall only
> while the Grandam stirs, in the storm season. Lit on a quiet night, it
> is only a light. See "The Truth of the Grandam".

## The North Coast

The cold side of the island, where the wind comes from the Wall and the
sea brings the wrecks.

### Gull Rock

*A white stack of rock off the north coast. Chapter 2.*

A tall stack of white rock a quarter of a mile off the north coast, loud
with ten thousand birds. At low tide, a ridge of slippery stone joins it
to the shore, and a climber can reach its foot. The climb to the top is
long and hard. The Tey folk climb it in spring for eggs, on ropes their
families have used for generations, and they are proud of it. A few
fall every year.

From the top of Gull Rock, a person sees farther than from anywhere else
on the island: the whole north sea, and the Wall itself, close enough to
see it move. Old Tey climbers say that the Wall "breathes" when you watch
it long enough. They are right.

**What the party finds.** Eggs, birds, danger, and the best view of the
Wall on Farholm.

### The Boneyard

*A beach on the north-west coast, deep in the timbers of old ships.
Chapter 2.*

A steep beach of black shingle where the currents of the north coast
leave everything that the sea breaks. Ribs of ships stand up out of the
stones like the bones of whales. Some timbers are older than the Landing,
from ships that nobody on Farholm has ever heard of. Some are from the
fleet. The Corrin men come here with barrows for wood and iron, and they
are the only people who do.

The Boneyard is a sad, loud place. The sea never stops, and the timbers
groan when it moves them. Children of Hythe say that the drowned sailors
of every lost ship walk here at night. Children are sometimes right.

**What the party finds.** Wood, iron, and the remains of every ship that
ever came to Farholm, including one that the Corrin men swear never came
back.

## The Interior

The middle of the island, where few colonists go: the warm springs, the
bare ridges, the wreck on the hills, and the holy places of the Tey folk.

### The Kettles

*Hot pools at the head of the Fleet. Chapter 2.*

About eight miles up the Fleet from Hythe, the valley ends in a bowl of
bare, yellow-stained ground full of steaming pools. Some are warm enough
to bathe in. Some boil. The air smells of eggs and iron, and the ground
is warm underfoot even in winter. The colonists come here to wash
clothes, to cook eggs in the hot pools, and, in winter, to sit in the
warm water and gossip. It is the one place on Farholm where Corrin men
and Tey folk share anything without a quarrel.

On still nights, the ground of the Kettles seems to rise and fall,
slowly, like the chest of a sleeper. The colonists say it is the steam
under the ground. This year, the pools are hotter than anyone remembers,
and two of them boil that never boiled before.

**What the party finds.** Warm water, gossip from both banks, and the
first clear sign that the ground breathes.

### The Ribs

*Long bare ridges of grey stone across the middle of the island. Chapter
2.*

West of the Kettles, the turf gives way to long, bare ridges of grey
stone that run north to south, one after another, like waves frozen in
place. The stone is ridged and pitted and strangely smooth, and nothing
grows on it but grey lichen. Between the ridges lie narrow valleys of
bog and black water. The walking is hard, the paths are few, and fog
gathers in the valleys every evening.

The colonists named the ridges the Ribs because they look like the ribs
of a great beast. They do not know how right they are.

### The wreck of the Morning Star

*The flagship of the old fleet, high on the Ribs. Chapter 2.*

High on a ridge of the Ribs, three miles from the nearest shore and
three hundred feet above the sea, lies the *Morning Star*. She sits
almost upright, with her keel in a groove of the stone, her masts gone
and her deck half fallen in. Grey lichen covers her timbers. In 360
years, she has not rotted as she should, because nothing on Farholm eats
wood up there.

The colonists say that the sea put her there on the night of the
Landing, when it stood up. Nobody goes inside. The Corrin men say it is
the Captain's ship and must not be robbed. The Tey folk say it is the
place where the Grandam first rose, and must not be disturbed. Both
sides agree that it is a bad place to be at night.

**What the party finds.** The oldest place of the fleet, untouched for
360 years: the captain's cabin, the charts of the voyage, and whatever
else the chapters put there.

> **Secret (chapter 2):** The sea did not put the *Morning Star* on the
> Ribs. The Grandam did. On the night of the Landing, she rose under the
> fleet, near to waking, and lifted the flagship on her back. When she
> settled, the ship stayed where she had put it.

### The Saint's Hearth

*A black hollow in the turf, in the west of the island. Chapter 2.*

A round hollow in the turf, about thirty feet across, where the ground
is black and soft as old ash and nothing grows. Snow does not lie in it,
and rain steams off it. In the middle stands a sun cross of driftwood,
taller than a man, hung with ribbons, shells, and the small gifts of the
Tey folk. This is where Saint Mawes lit his fire.

The Tey folk walk here on Saint Mawes's Day, at midwinter, and stand in
a ring around the hollow in the dark, with no light at all, and sing the
dawn hymn. No Tey person ever steps into the hollow. Most Corrin men stay
away from it too. Nobody admits that they are afraid.

**What the party finds.** The place of the legend, a place of real
power, and a warm, soft, living ground under the ash.

> **Secret (chapter 2):** The ground of the Saint's Hearth is the
> Grandam's skin, burned thin by the saint's fire 360 years ago. A hand
> pressed flat on it, in silence, feels a heartbeat, very slow and very
> deep: one beat in a minute.

### The Grandam's Well

*The holy well of the Tey folk, in the south of the island. Chapters 2
and 3.*

In a fold of the hills between the Ribs and the south coast, a round
shaft opens in the ground: twelve feet across, with walls of wet black
stone, going down farther than any rope in Hythe. Warm air rises from
it, slowly, in and out, every few minutes. The Tey folk built a low wall
of white stones around it and a roof of turf over it, and they call it
the Grandam's Well.

Once a week, the Well Keeper leads the Tey folk here at dusk. They sing
the dawn hymn of the saint and drop small gifts into the dark: eggs,
ribbons, coins, and, in the past, pieces of the broken lamp of the Corrin
Light. Nobody has ever come back up the shaft. Nobody of the Tey folk
would try. The Well Watch keeps outsiders away.

**What the party finds.** The holy place of the Tey folk, the way to the
heart of the Grandam, and, far down, the gifts of three hundred years.

> **Secret (chapter 3):** The Well goes down to the Grandam's heart. It
> is her breathing hole, or something like one. A person who climbs down
> far enough, past the gifts, comes at last to warm, soft walls that move,
> and to a sound like the sea in a shell, that is her heart. The last
> chapter takes the party there.

## Life on Farholm

The small facts of the colony. Use them to make every scene in Hythe feel
like a place that kept old Ostrey in a bottle for 360 years.

### Speech

The colonists speak the language of old Ostrey, slowly and formally,
full of sea words. They think the party talks very fast and very
roughly, like drunken sailors. They say "thee" to children and close
friends, and "you" to everyone else.

| On Farholm | What it means |
|---|---|
| "Light to you." "And dawn to you." | The greeting of old Ostrey. The colonists say it to everyone. |
| "Dark take it." | The small curse of old Ostrey. |
| The Wall | The wall of storms around the island. |
| The Turning | The storm season, when the Wall comes in. |
| The Landing | The day the fleet came ashore. Year 0. |
| Over the Wall | The land of home. It also means "never", as in "I'll pay you back when I'm over the Wall." |
| The Long Count | The Tey folk's count of the Grandam's long sleep. |
| Middle arch | A fair deal, or a fair fight. |
| A Corrin man, a Tey woman | A person of that bank, even if their family has married across. |

When a colonist hears the party's greeting of today, "Wake well", they
look puzzled. "Wake who?"

### Money

The fleet brought chests of coin: gold suns, silver stars, and copper
bits, with the head of King Edwy, who was the king of Ostrey in the year
of the sailing. Ten bits make a star, and ten stars make a sun. In 360
years, most of the gold went into the ground with the dead or into the
Well with the gifts. A family with a gold sun keeps it in a box and shows
it to guests.

Today, most trade in Hythe is in salt, fish, eggs, and work. A day's work
is worth about a bit. Bits are cut from the copper sheathing nails of
the wrecks. A star buys a sheep. Nobody in Hythe has seen a gold piece of
today, and a coin of Corrantey, with its lamp, is a marvel. A colonist
will trade almost anything for one.

The party's own goods are worth far more than their coins. To a colonist,
these are treasures:

- iron of any kind: nails, a knife, a needle
- glass: a bottle, a lens, a mirror
- white flour, sugar, spice, wine, or tea
- new cloth, especially colored cloth
- paper and ink
- a book, any book, from over the Wall

### Food and drink

The colonists eat what the island and the sea give. Fish of every kind,
dried, salted, smoked, and boiled. Seal meat and seal oil. Sea birds and
their eggs from Gull Rock. Limpets and mussels from the rocks. Laver
bread, a black paste of boiled seaweed, eaten with everything. Barley
bread, barley porridge, and barley ale, from small fields on the slopes
above Hythe. Mutton from the small brown sheep of Founders' Hill, on feast
days only.

They have no wine, no spice, no sugar, and no tea. They make a strong,
bitter drink from heather and call it heather ale. A guest who drinks it
without a face makes a friend.

### Dress

The clothes of Farholm are the clothes of old Ostrey, cut the same way
for 360 years and made of different stuff: wool from the small sheep,
sealskin, and old sailcloth. Men wear long coats and wide breeches.
Women wear long skirts, shawls, and white caps. Everything is patched.
A garment that came from the fleet is an heirloom, worn on Landing Day
only.

On holy days, each side wears a ribbon of its old town's color. Corrin
is a white ship on blue, and the Corrin men wear blue ribbons. Tey is a
silver fish on green, and the Tey folk wear green.

### Customs that keep old Ostrey alive

- **The dawn fire.** The priest lights it every morning in the church.
  Many families light a small one on their own hearth at dawn and say
  "Light to you" to the first person they see.
- **The hearth stones.** No fire burns on the bare ground of Farholm.
  Every hearth in Hythe stands on ballast stones from the ships. A cook
  fire on a trip into the interior is built on a flat stone carried for
  the purpose. The Tey folk keep this custom strictly. The Corrin men keep
  it from habit, and the young ones laugh at it. A stranger who lights a
  fire on the bare turf horrifies everyone who sees it.
- **The east window.** Many Corrin families keep a small lamp in an east
  window at night, as the Lamp Room does, "so that home can find us".
- **The Longest Night.** At midwinter, each house keeps one candle
  burning until dawn, as in old Ostrey. Saint Mawes's Day is the next
  night, and the Tey folk spend it in the dark at the Saint's Hearth.
- **Landing Day.** The great feast, at midsummer. The whole colony eats
  together on Founders' Hill, both banks at one long table, for one day.
  Father Rundle reads the names of the eleven ships. Every year, someone
  starts a fight before dark.
- **The ships' names.** Every family knows which ship it came from and
  says so. "We are *Dove* people." A person of the *Morning Star* or the
  *Patience* is a little proud of it.
- **The old songs.** The colonists sing the songs of old Ostrey, the
  songs of Corrin and Tey, and the songs of the voyage. A party that
  knows an old song of the land, even a changed one, hears a whole room
  go quiet to listen.
- **The Moot oath.** A promise made on the middle arch of the Bridge, in
  front of witnesses, is binding. Breaking it is the worst thing a
  colonist can do.

## The Truth of the Grandam

This section is the secret of the book. The players must not learn it
all before chapter 3. Pieces of it come out in chapter 2, through the
sites, Inga's dreams, Nathaniel Gale's notes, and the wreck on the Ribs.

### What she is

Farholm is the back of a living creature. She is a beast of the deep
sea, older than Ostrey, as big as the island, sleeping at the surface
of the Western Sea. The island is the crust of her back: stone, sand,
shell, and turf that built up on her over centuries of sleep. The Ribs
are the ridges of her back. The Kettles are near the place where she
breathes. The Grandam's Well goes down to her heart.

She drifts very slowly with the currents, a few miles in a year. This
is why no chart shows Farholm.

She is not a god. She does not know the colonists exist. She has never
punished anyone, and she has never loved anyone. She is an animal, vast
and slow and old, asleep. But she is alive, and what the Tey folk feel
in the ground is real.

### The Wall

The Wall is her breath. She breathes out under the sea, slowly, a warm
breath that rises through the water about twenty miles out and meets the
cold air of the open sea. The warm air and the cold air make a ring of
storms around her that never stops. When she breathes in, the wind blows
toward her from every side. This is why every ship that comes near the
Wall goes in with the wind and wrecks on the island. No ship has ever
sailed out against it.

### The storm season

Each autumn, she stirs in her sleep. She breathes harder and faster,
and the Wall comes in over the island. This is the Turning. For a few
weeks, the storms are on Farholm itself. The colonists shut their doors,
tie down their roofs, and wait. Each year, a little more of the coast
falls into the sea.

### The Long Count

Once in about 360 years, she does more than stir. She rises toward
waking. The last time was the Landing: on the night the fleet came in,
she rose under the ships and set the *Morning Star* on her back. Then
she settled again.

This year is the next time. The Tey folk have kept the Long Count since
Agnes Flett, and Elspeth Flett has worked out that it ends this year.
She has told nobody but the Net Loft. The signs are everywhere: the
boiling Kettles, the breathing ground, Inga Halcrow's dreams, and the
Wall drawing in early. The Storm Cube measures this. At 64, she begins
to wake.

### The Light and the path

She rises toward a great light, as a fish rises to a lantern. While she
rises, she holds her breath, and the Wall on the side of the light goes
quiet. That quiet is the path: a lane of calm water, straight out from
the light, with stars over it.

This only works while she stirs. On a quiet night, she sleeps too deep
to see any light, and the Corrin Light is only a lamp on a tower. In the
storm season, she sees it. In this year of all years, she sees it best.

So the Light opens the only way home, and it opens it at the worst
possible time. Every hour it burns, she rises a little more. John Pascoe
had the path for one night. The Tey folk put the lamp out, and she
settled, and the path closed.

### If she wakes

If she wakes fully, she dives within a few days. The island goes under
the sea. The Wall breaks up into ordinary storms and blows away, and the
Western Sea is open for the first time in an age. Any person on the
island when she dives drowns, unless they are on a boat well away from
her.

### If she sleeps again

If she settles, she sleeps for another long sleep. The Wall stays. The
path closes. The Light can open it again only in a later storm season,
with the same danger, and the next Long Count is 360 years away.

The way to put her back to sleep is in the legend of the saint. Put out
every fire on her back. Go down to her heart in the dark. Be quiet with
her until she settles. The Tey folk's hymn is not magic, but it helps a
frightened singer to stay calm, and calm is what the heart needs. The
last chapter gives the rules.

### Who is right

Neither side, and both.

- **The Corrin men** are right that the Light opens a path. They are
  wrong that it works at any time, wrong about the *Hopewell*, and wrong
  about home. Corrin waits for nobody.
- **The Tey folk** are right that the Grandam is alive and that fire
  wakes her. They are wrong that she loves them, wrong that leaving
  wakes her, and wrong that the Light caused the Drowning. The storm
  season did.

The party is the first people in 360 years who can see the whole truth.
What they do with it is the last chapter.

### The news to take home

If the party reaches Corrantey, they bring the first true news from
beyond the Western Sea. Nobody has come back before. The Lampwrights,
the council of the city, the sailors of the docks, and the old families
who still say "my people were Corrin" or "my people were Tey" will all
want to hear it.

What the party says matters. A living island and a lost colony is a
wonder. It is also a story that can start a hundred voyages, or stop
them forever. The last chapter gives the results of each choice.
