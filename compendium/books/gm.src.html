Your Job as Game Master

You are the world, every character in it except the heroes, and the referee. You are not the players' opponent. You are the person making sure everyone has a good story to tell afterwards.

The Core Loop

Every moment of the game, in or out of combat, is this loop:

  1. Describe the scene. Two or three sensory details, not a paragraph.
  2. Ask: "What do you do?"
  3. Players describe what their characters try.
  4. Decide: does it simply work, simply fail, or is the outcome uncertain? If uncertain and failure would be interesting, call for a roll.
  5. Narrate the result. Then back to step 1.

Your Three Hats

Narrator

Describes what the characters see, hear and smell. Sets tone and pace.

Every NPC

Voices the innkeeper, the villain, the dragon. Gives each a want and one memorable quirk.

Referee

Decides when to roll, sets difficulty, interprets rules, keeps it fair.

Before Your First Session

  • Run a published adventure first. The Starter Set adventures, or a free one-shot, teach you pacing. Your own world comes in campaign two.
  • Use pre-made characters for session one, or run a dedicated character creation session (Session Zero).
  • Learn only: the d20 test (Calling for Rolls), advantage (Advantage, Disadvantage & Difficulty), and the combat turn (Running Combat). Everything else can be looked up.
  • Prep light: see Session Prep. One page of notes is plenty.

Session Zero

A short conversation before the campaign begins. Spend 15–30 minutes agreeing on:

  • Tone: lighthearted adventure, grim survival, political intrigue, horror?
  • Boundaries: content anyone wants kept off the table (lines), or kept "off-screen" (veils).
  • Party: why do these characters stick together? Give them one shared reason.
  • Logistics: how often you play, how long, food, phones, what happens if someone misses a session.
  • Table rules: do dice that fall off the table count? (Usually: reroll.)

Principles of Good GMing

  • Say yes, or "yes, but". If a plan is creative and plausible, let it work, maybe with a cost.
  • Failure moves the story forward. A failed lockpick isn't "nothing happens"; it's "the lock clicks open, but a guard heard it."
  • Spotlight everyone. Quiet players get asked directly: "What's your character doing while this happens?"
  • Fun over accuracy. You can fudge your own dice behind the screen. Use this rarely and only to make the game better.
  • Telegraph danger. Give hints before deadly things happen: bones in the cave, a scorch mark on the floor. Deaths should feel earned, not random.
  • Rule of cool. When a rule and an amazing moment conflict, consider the amazing moment.

For Writers

You already have the hardest skills: worldbuilding, voices, description. The adjustment is that you are no longer the author of the plot: the players co-write it in real time.

Habits to Unlearn

Writer habitAt the table instead
Plotting the storyPrep situations, not plots: who wants what, and what happens if nobody interferes. Let the players decide what to do about it.
Lore dumpsReveal lore only when asked, discovered, or needed: one or two sentences at a time. Your full history is for you.
Protecting darlingsIf they kill your villain in session two, let it stand. Someone even angrier picks up the mantle.
Narrating characters' inner livesNever say what a player character feels, thinks or decides. Describe the world; they own their heroes.
Planned twistsHold reveals loosely. If players guess the twist early, reward them. Don't swap the twist to stay clever.
Long descriptionsTwo or three vivid details, then "What do you do?" Players' attention lives in action.

Strengths to Lean On

  • Reincorporation. Bring back what players did earlier: the NPC they spared, the lie they told, the bridge they burned. This makes the campaign feel authored without railroading.
  • Character hooks. Ask each player for a backstory secret, a person they care about, and an unfinished goal. Weave all three into your world.
  • NPC voice. One verbal tic + one want per NPC. Write them on an index card.
  • Pacing instinct. End sessions on a cliffhanger. Start sessions with action.

The Lazy GM Prep Method

A widely used eight-step checklist (from Sly Flourish's Return of the Lazy Dungeon Master). Do only the steps you need:

  1. Review the characters: what does each player want?
  2. A strong start: open with something happening.
  3. Potential scenes: 3–5 things that might happen. Not a sequence.
  4. Secrets and clues: 10 short facts players could discover. Not tied to locations. Drop them wherever players go.
  5. Fantastic locations: each with three evocative details.
  6. Important NPCs: name, want, quirk.
  7. Relevant monsters: stat blocks ready.
  8. Magic item rewards: what could they find?

The mindset shift

You are running a living world, not telling a story. The story is what everyone remembers afterwards, and you'll be the best person at the table to write it down.

Calling for Rolls

Almost every roll in the game is a d20 Test: roll a twenty-sided die, add a modifier, compare to a target number.

The d20 Test

d20 + ability modifier + proficiency bonus (if trained) ≥ target number = success

When to Call for a Roll

Ask yourself two questions:

  1. Is the outcome uncertain? Opening an unlocked door: no roll. Leaping a 30-foot chasm: maybe impossible, no roll either.
  2. Is failure interesting? If failing just means "try again," let them succeed (or take longer).

Only roll when both answers are yes.

Choosing the Ability

Players describe what they do; you choose which ability and skill applies. Never let players lead with "Can I roll Perception?" Ask "What do you do?"

Mixing abilities with skills

Skills are suggestions, not locks. Intimidating someone by lifting them off the ground can be a Strength (Intimidation) check. Recalling which wild berries are safe can be Wisdom (Nature). If two abilities fit, choose the one that matches the method.

Contests, Passive Checks & Group Checks

  • Contest: both sides roll; higher wins. Ties keep the situation as it was. (Stealth vs. Perception, arm-wrestling, grapple escapes.)
  • Passive check: 10 + the character's modifiers, used when you don't want to tip players off by asking for a roll. Passive Perception is on every sheet. Compare hidden things against it silently.
  • Group check: everyone rolls; if at least half succeed, the group succeeds. Great for sneaking as a party or navigating a swamp.
  • Working together: one character takes the Help action → the other rolls with advantage.

Advantage, Disadvantage & Difficulty

Two dials you control: the DC sets how hard the task is; advantage or disadvantage reflects the circumstances.

Difficulty Classes

DCDifficultyExample
5Very easyClimbing a knotted rope. (Often skip the roll.)
10EasySpotting an obvious track, calming a nervous horse
15MediumPicking a decent lock, climbing a rough cliff
20HardSwimming a raging river, lying to a suspicious noble
25Very hardLeaping between rooftops in a storm
30Nearly impossibleConvincing the king to abdicate on the spot

Tell players the DC or keep it secret; both are fine. Many GMs announce it so players can make informed choices.

Advantage & Disadvantage

Advantage

Roll two d20s, use the higher. Worth roughly +3 to +5. Crit chance rises from 5% to about 10%.

Disadvantage

Roll two d20s, use the lower. Worth roughly −3 to −5.

  • They never stack. Three sources of advantage = one advantage.
  • They cancel. Any advantage + any disadvantage = a normal roll, no matter how many of each.
  • Advantage is all-or-nothing. You only decide whether, never how much.

When to Grant Advantage

  • An ally used the Help action
  • The target is Prone (melee), Restrained, Stunned, Paralyzed, Unconscious, or can't see the attacker
  • The attacker is hidden or Invisible
  • A creative setup clearly helps: sand in the eyes, the right tool, research done earlier
  • Roleplay that targets what an NPC genuinely cares about

When to Impose Disadvantage

  • Long-range attacks, or a ranged attack with an enemy right next to you
  • The attacker is Blinded, Poisoned, Frightened, Prone or Restrained
  • Harsh conditions: darkness, storms, ice, a rocking ship
  • A poor approach: lying to someone who already knows the truth

Rules of thumb

  • The "obviously" test: would this obviously make it easier or harder? If you have to argue, don't grant it.
  • Task vs. circumstance: a sheer cliff is a higher DC. Climbing it with a rope is advantage.
  • Don't overuse it. If players have advantage on everything, it stops feeling special.
  • Reward creativity. It's your easiest tool for making clever ideas pay off.

Heroic Inspiration

A reward you can give for great roleplay, clever ideas or heroic sacrifice. A player with Heroic Inspiration can spend it to reroll any one die and use the new roll. A character either has it or doesn't (it doesn't stack). Humans start every day with it. Hand it out about once per player per session.

Running Combat

Combat is the most rules-heavy part of the game. Learn this chapter well; it covers 90% of what you'll look up.

Step by Step

  1. Establish positions. Where is everyone? (Use a map, a sketch, or describe it: "theatre of the mind.")
  2. Roll initiative. Everyone rolls d20 + Dexterity modifier. You roll once per group of identical monsters.
  3. Order from highest to lowest. Ties: players decide among themselves; for player vs. monster, you decide.
  4. Take turns. Each creature acts on its turn. When everyone has gone, that's one round (about 6 seconds).
  5. Repeat rounds in the same order until one side is defeated, flees or surrenders.

Surprise

If a creature is caught off guard by an ambush, it rolls initiative with disadvantage. That's the whole rule in 2024.

Making an Attack

  1. Roll to hit: d20 + attack bonus vs. target's AC. Meet or beat = hit.
  2. Roll damage: weapon/spell dice + ability modifier. Subtract from HP.
  • Natural 20: automatic hit and Critical Hit: roll all damage dice twice, add modifiers once.
  • Natural 1: automatic miss.
  • Melee uses Strength (or Dex for Finesse weapons). Ranged uses Dexterity.
  • Ranged attacks beyond normal range (up to long range) have disadvantage, as do ranged attacks while an enemy is within 5 ft.
  • Spell attacks work the same: d20 + spell attack bonus vs. AC.
  • Saving throw spells flip it: the target rolls a save vs. the caster's spell save DC. Fail = full effect; many spells do half damage on a success.

Opportunity Attacks

When a creature you can see leaves your reach using its movement, you can use your reaction to make one melee attack against it. Disengage, teleporting, or being moved by someone else avoids it.

Grapple & Shove

Both replace one attack with an Unarmed Strike option. The target makes a Strength or Dexterity save (its choice) against DC 8 + your Strength modifier + proficiency bonus.

  • Grapple: target is Grappled (Speed 0). It can use its action to try escaping (Athletics or Acrobatics vs. the same DC).
  • Shove: push the target 5 ft away, or knock it Prone.

Damage Types, Resistance & Vulnerability

Damage types: Acid, Bludgeoning, Cold, Fire, Force, Lightning, Necrotic, Piercing, Poison, Psychic, Radiant, Slashing, Thunder.

  • Resistance: take half damage of that type.
  • Vulnerability: take double.
  • Immunity: take none.

Running Combat Smoothly

  • Visible initiative order. Write it on a whiteboard or drape index cards over your screen.
  • Announce "on deck": "Nilah, you're up; mage, you're next."
  • Describe hits by margin. Barely hit = a scratch through armor. Huge hit = cinematic.
  • Let players describe killing blows. "How do you want to do this?"
  • Monsters flee or surrender when badly hurt. Most creatures want to live, and it shortens drawn-out fights.
  • Track monster HP on scrap paper; tell players "bloodied" when a monster is below half.
  • Vary objectives: rescue the hostage, hold the door for three rounds, stop the ritual. Not every fight is "kill everything."

Hit Points, Death & Resting

Hit Points

HP represents stamina, luck and the will to keep fighting as much as physical wounds. Losing HP doesn't have to mean bleeding until the last few points.

  • Maximum HP: grows with level. Current HP can't exceed it.
  • Temporary HP: a buffer that's lost first. Doesn't stack (keep the higher amount), can't be healed, disappears on a long rest.
  • Bloodied: at half HP or less. Not a rule by itself, but useful to describe.

Dropping to 0 Hit Points

Monsters & NPCs

Die (or fall unconscious, if the attacker chooses a nonlethal melee blow).

Player characters

Fall Unconscious and begin making death saving throws.

Death Saving Throws

  • At the start of each of their turns, the character rolls a d20 (no modifiers).
  • 10 or higher: one success. 9 or lower: one failure.
  • Three successes: stable (unconscious, but no longer dying). Three failures: dead.
  • Natural 20: regain 1 HP and wake up. Natural 1: counts as two failures.
  • Taking damage while at 0 HP: one failure (two if it's a Critical Hit).
  • Any healing brings them back up with that much HP, conscious.
  • Stabilizing: an ally can use an action and succeed on a DC 10 Wisdom (Medicine) check.

Instant Death

If damage reduces a character to 0 HP and the remaining damage equals or exceeds their HP maximum, they die instantly. (Rare outside of low levels.)

Resting

Short Rest: 1 hour

  • Spend any number of Hit Dice: roll each, add Con modifier, heal that much.
  • Some features recharge (Action Surge, Warlock slots, Second Wind uses, etc.).

Long Rest: 8 hours

  • Regain all HP and all spent Hit Dice.
  • Regain all spell slots and most features.
  • Remove 1 level of Exhaustion.
  • Only one long rest per 24 hours. Interrupted by an hour of combat/walking/spellcasting.

Hit Dice

Each character has one Hit Die per level, sized by class: d6 (Sorcerer, Wizard), d8 (Bard, Cleric, Druid, Monk, Rogue, Warlock), d10 (Fighter, Paladin, Ranger), d12 (Barbarian). They're the party's built-in healing reserve. When they run low, the party must decide whether to push on or rest. That tension is good.

Levels, XP & Progression

Two Ways to Level Up

Milestone (recommended)

You announce "you level up" at the end of a major story beat. Nothing to track. Ties progression to story, not kill counts. Perfect for a writer's table.

Experience Points

Characters earn XP for defeating monsters (see Building Encounters) and level up at thresholds. Rewards combat more than other play.

Pacing Suggestions

  • Level 1 → 2: after the first session.
  • Level 2 → 3: after 1–2 more sessions (subclasses unlock at 3, and players love this).
  • Levels 3–5: every 2–3 sessions.
  • Level 5+: every 3–4 sessions, or at major arc endings.

What Players Gain on Level Up

Players handle this themselves using their class table. Briefly: more max HP (Hit Die or fixed average + Con), new class features, a subclass at level 3, an Ability Score Improvement or feat at levels 4/8/12/16 (Fighters and Rogues get extras), a higher proficiency bonus at 5/9/13/17, and more spells for casters. Ask players to tell you about any big new abilities so you can plan around them.

Encounter Design Tips

  • Watch the action economy. One big monster vs. four heroes gets swarmed. Add minions so the boss isn't alone.
  • Avoid monsters with CR higher than the party's level at levels 1–4. A single hit can kill.
  • Terrain matters: a ledge, a collapsing bridge, cover, darkness. Interesting places make interesting fights.
  • Give monsters goals other than "fight to the death": steal the artifact, escape, protect the egg.
  • Expect 2–4 encounters per long rest as a rough guide; one huge fight per day lets casters burn everything.
  • It's okay to adjust mid-fight: reinforcements arrive, or the monster has fewer HP than written.

Movement & Physical Feats

  • Climbing, swimming, crawling: each foot costs 1 extra foot of movement (unless the creature has a Climb/Swim Speed).
  • Long jump: up to your Strength score in feet with a 10-ft running start (half without).
  • High jump: 3 + Strength modifier feet with a running start (half without).
  • Falling: 1d6 Bludgeoning per 10 ft fallen, maximum 20d6. Land Prone if you take damage.
  • Holding breath: a number of minutes equal to 1 + Con modifier (minimum 30 seconds).
  • Carrying: up to Strength × 15 lb. Lift/push/drag up to double that (at reduced speed).

Social Interaction

NPCs have attitudes: Friendly, Indifferent or Hostile. Roleplay first; call for a Charisma check only when the outcome is uncertain. A good argument can grant advantage or let it succeed without a roll. A Friendly NPC helps with reasonable requests; a Hostile one won't take risks for the party at all.

Languages

Everyone speaks Common plus a couple of others. Standard languages: Common, Common Sign Language, Draconic, Dwarvish, Elvish, Giant, Gnomish, Goblin, Halfling, Orc. Rare languages include Abyssal, Celestial, Deep Speech, Druidic, Infernal, Primordial, Sylvan, Thieves' Cant and Undercommon.

Session Prep

Before Each Session (30–60 min)

  1. Re-read last session's notes. What did players care about?
  2. Write a strong start: open on action or a decision.
  3. Jot 3–5 possible scenes. Don't sequence them.
  4. Write 10 secrets and clues. Cross them off as players find them.
  5. Name and quirk any new NPCs.
  6. Prepare stat blocks for likely fights; mark their HP on scrap paper.
  7. Prepare one reward or reveal.

Running the Session

  • Recap: ask a player to recap last session (it shows you what they remember).
  • Breaks: one break every ~90 minutes.
  • Ending: stop on a cliffhanger or a decision, not mid-fight if you can help it.
  • After: spend 5 minutes writing what happened, unresolved threads, and who players liked.

Running Marks

Marks turn curses, blessings and temptations into rules. The players' side is in the Marks chapter of the Player's Compendium. This chapter is yours: how to give Marks, how to keep hidden ones, how to run the crisis, and the Taints, whose prices the players shouldn't see.

Before You Start

Talk to the table before you turn Marks on. Hidden Marks especially need consent: some players love not knowing, and some hate it. Ask in your first session, and agree on these points:

  • Are hidden Marks allowed? You can make every Mark open if the table prefers it.
  • Can the story put a Curse on a character with no choice? Most tables say yes; a few prefer bargains only.
  • Which themes are off the table? Some Sins touch subjects that some players don't want at the table. Leave those Taints out.

Giving a Mark

From the story. Give a Mark when something big happens to a character: they defy a god, drink from the wrong spring, carry a dying saint's last words. A Mark should feel earned or deserved, never random.

From a bargain. When a player goes looking for power, decide who answers and what they want. Witches, fallen saints, the Sins themselves, a fey lord with a long memory. Set the price before you make the offer. With a hidden Mark you can keep part of the price secret, but never lie about the part you state.

One Mark at a time

One Mark per character is plenty for most campaigns. Two is a lot. Three should feel like a character at the edge of something.

Keeping Hidden Marks

Keep a card for each hidden Mark: its name, the character, the track and the thresholds. When the trigger happens, add 1 (at most once each scene, unless the Mark says otherwise) and describe a sign that the whole table can see. Signs should be clear enough to notice and vague enough to misread. When a threshold changes the character's numbers, tell the player the new number, not the reason.

When the track reaches 5, reveal the Mark. Hand the player the full text, then run the crisis.

Running the Crisis

The crisis is a scene, not a roll. Give it a moment of its own, ideally at a high point of the story. Then let the player choose: give in or break free. Don't push. Both choices should be good stories.

When a character gives in, you steer them only at the key moments that the Mark names. Keep those moments short: say what the character does or chooses in one or two sentences, then give the character back. Never use a Mark to kill a character, make them attack an ally to the death, or leave the party for good. A player loses a character only by choice.

When a character breaks free, describe the scar and let the moment land. Breaking free should feel like a victory, even if it cost something.

Pacing

A Mark is a story arc. A good pace is one threshold every two or three sessions, which brings the crisis near the end of an adventure. If a track fills too fast, the trigger is too common: let the growth wait for big moments only, or for one scene in each session. If it fills too slowly, offer more temptation.

Power and Encounters

Marks make characters stronger, and they don't cost a feat or a class feature. Count them when you build encounters:

  • Before its crisis, a Mark's benefit is worth about an Uncommon magic item.
  • After a character gives in, the full power is worth about a Rare magic item.

If the party carries more Marks than it has characters, build encounters as if the party had one more character. If two or more characters have given in to a Mark, also use monsters of 1 CR more where you can.

Making Your Own Marks

Use the Marks in this book as models. A new Mark needs a benefit (a Curse can skip it), a cost that matters in play, a clear trigger, one to three thresholds, a full power for giving in, a small scar, and two ways to end it: one before the crisis and a harder one after giving in. Keep the benefit near an Uncommon item and the full power near a Rare one. Name the key moments when you steer the character, and keep them narrow.

Running Bonds

Bonds put the friendships and rivalries of the party into rules. The players' side is in the Bonds chapter of the Player's Compendium. This chapter tells you how to grow Bonds, how to strain them fairly, and how to keep the fights fair when they are on.

Starting Bonds

You can start a campaign with one Bond for each character: ask each player to name another character and a kind, and tell one sentence of how the Bond began. Or let Bonds form in play, after the first scenes that earn them. Both ways work. The second is slower, and each Bond means more.

A Bond with an NPC is a promise from you: that NPC matters, and will be back. Give one only to an NPC you plan to keep in the story. You spend the Bond for the NPC, so play it as the NPC would: a mentor lends a hand, a rival waits for the right moment.

Defining Scenes

A defining scene tests a Bond or shows what it means. It doesn't need to be a fight. Good examples: one partner risks something real for the other; the two disagree, and work it out; a Rival admits the other was better, this time; a Debt is partly paid at a cost. Grow the die at the end of the scene, and say why. At most once each session per Bond keeps the dice from growing too fast.

Strain Without Cruelty

Strain is for betrayal, not for bad luck. A character who fails to save a friend hasn't strained the Bond; a character who runs and leaves the friend behind has. When you think a Bond might strain, say so before the choice, not after: "If you leave her here, that strains your Bond." Let the player decide with open eyes.

A Bond between two player characters is also a bond between two players. Never push a player to betray another player's character. Strain should come from choices the players want to make, for their own reasons.

Power and Encounters

Bonds make the party stronger, and they don't cost a feat or a class feature. A d10 Bond adds about 5 to one roll or prevents about 10 damage once each Short Rest. Count them like this:

  • While most Bonds in the party have a d4 or a d6, change nothing.
  • When most Bonds have a d8 or a d10, add one monster of the encounter's lowest CR for each four characters, or give each important enemy 10% more Hit Points.

If you also use Marks, count these two changes separately from the ones in Running Marks.

Running Techniques

Techniques give every character a few extra choices in each fight. The players' side is in the Techniques chapter of the Player's Compendium. This chapter tells you what they change at the table, and how to keep the fights fair when they are on.

What Changes

Techniques make fights a little longer to run and a lot more interesting to play. Each character has a few more choices on each turn, and the classes that had the fewest choices (the ones without spells) gain the most. Expect each character to use two or three techniques between Short Rests.

Techniques reward Short Rests. If your adventures rarely allow one, characters run out of points early in the day. Offer a Short Rest after most hard fights, or use this optional rule: when a fight ends, each character regains half their technique points (round down, minimum 1).

Monsters and NPCs

Monsters don't use techniques. An important NPC can: give a warrior NPC two techniques and technique points equal to its Proficiency Bonus. Don't give an NPC more techniques than a character of its level would know.

Power and Encounters

Techniques make characters stronger, and they don't cost a feat or a class feature. A technique point is worth about one extra die of weapon damage or one small effect, such as pushing a creature or giving an ally Advantage. Count them like this:

  • At levels 1 to 4, change nothing. Two points don't change much.
  • At level 5 or more, add one monster of the encounter's lowest CR for each four characters, or give each important enemy 10% more Hit Points.

If you also use other optional systems, count each system's changes separately.

Making Your Own Techniques

A 1-point technique should be about as strong as one extra die of weapon damage. A 2-point technique can add a condition or affect two creatures. A 3-point technique, at level 11 or more, can be as strong as a spell of level 3. Stances should give a steady small benefit and take a small cost, or none. Don't copy a class feature: a technique should feel like training that anyone could learn.

Running Legend Paths

Legend paths reward the characters for the kind of hero they choose to be. The players' side is in the Legend Paths chapter of the Player's Compendium. This chapter tells you how to give legend points, how to pace the tiers, and how to keep the fights fair at the top.

Giving Legend Points

A deed earns a point when it is big, when it fits the path, and when people will hear of it. Killing a dragon earns a Dragonslayer point; killing a lizard doesn't. Giving away a fortune earns a Saint point; giving a beggar a coin doesn't. When you are not sure, ask the table: "Will the bards sing about this?"

Say it aloud when a point is earned, and say why. Legend points work best when the whole table sees them happen.

Pacing

A good pace is one legend point every two sessions. At that pace, a character reaches Tier V near the end of a long campaign. If your campaign is short, give a point every session. Remember that tiers also need a level: points never let a character run ahead of their level.

The Fall

Use the fall the way you use strain on a Bond: warn the player before the choice, not after. "If you take the bribe, that's the Saint's fall." A fall is a story moment, not a punishment for a bad roll.

Power and Encounters

Legend paths make characters stronger, and they don't cost a feat or a class feature. Each tier is worth about a magic item: Tier I a Common item, Tier II an Uncommon one, Tier III a Rare one, Tier IV a Very Rare one, and Tier V a Legendary one. Count them like this:

  • Tiers I and II: change nothing.
  • When most characters have reached Tier III: build encounters as if the party were one level higher.
  • When most characters have reached Tier V: build encounters as if the party were two levels higher, and give important enemies Legendary Actions.

If you also use other optional systems, count each system's changes separately.

Making Your Own Paths

A path needs a clear kind of deed, a clear fall, and five tiers that grow from a small trick to a power that changes how the character plays. Tie each tier to the path's story: a Dragonslayer's benefits work against dragons, and a Wanderer's work on the road. Keep each tier near the magic item rarity above.

Injuries and Scars

Hit Points come back after a night's sleep. Injuries don't. With this system on, a critical hit or a fall to 0 Hit Points can leave a broken arm, a cracked rib or a ringing in the ears that stays until it heals, and a healed injury leaves a scar that the character carries with pride. This chapter has the whole system: the players don't need a chapter of their own.

Before You Start

Injuries make the game grittier, and they are optional. The GM and the table agree to use them before the campaign starts. Some groups love a fighter who limps through the next dungeon; others want their heroes whole by morning. Agree on these points:

  • Who can be injured? The usual answer is the player characters and the NPCs that matter. Ordinary monsters just die.
  • Is the Lost Eye allowed to be for good? The player always chooses, but some tables leave permanent losses out entirely.

When an Injury Happens

A character risks an injury in two moments:

  • a creature scores a Critical Hit against them, or
  • they drop to 0 Hit Points and aren't killed outright.

If both happen on the same hit, it is one injury, not two. Critical Hits against a character give an injury only once in each turn, however many land. Then the player makes a choice.

The Player's Choice

Before anything is rolled, the player chooses one:

  • Take the injury. Roll on the Injury table.
  • Shake it off. The character gains 1 Exhaustion level instead, and no injury.

An injury never kills a character, and it never takes a character out of the game. The worst injuries slow a character down for a few weeks. A permanent loss happens only when the player chooses it.

Rolling the Injury

Roll a d20 on the Injury table. If the character dropped to 0 Hit Points, add 5 to the roll; a total above 20 counts as 20. A critical hit alone mostly gives Minor injuries; a fall to 0 Hit Points gives the bad ones.

Each injury says what it does and how it heals. A character can carry at most three injuries. If a fourth comes, the player chooses which injury it replaces, and the replaced injury heals with no scar.

Healing

Restoring Hit Points doesn't heal an injury. Each injury names its own treatment. In short:

  • Minor injuries heal after a Long Rest, some only after someone tends them.
  • Serious injuries need several Long Rests with care, or a Lesser Restoration spell.
  • Lasting injuries need a month of rest as a downtime activity, or a Greater Restoration, Heal or Regenerate spell.

Scars

When an injury heals with rest and care, not with magic, the character gains its scar: a small, permanent benefit and a story to tell at the fire. A character can have a number of scars up to their Proficiency Bonus. When they would gain one more, the player can swap an old scar for the new one, or keep the old one.

That is the trade at the heart of the system. Magic heals at once and leaves nothing. Time heals slowly and leaves the character a little tougher.

Power and Encounters

Injuries make characters weaker for a while, and scars make them a little stronger for good. Over a campaign they roughly cancel out. Count them like this:

  • While characters carry injuries, don't add to the danger. Give an injured party a real chance to rest, and don't stack hard fights on the same day.
  • When most characters have three or more scars, give each important enemy 10% more Hit Points.

If you also use other optional systems, count each system's changes separately.

Making Your Own Injuries

A Minor injury costs one small thing, such as a few feet of Speed or Disadvantage on one kind of check. A Serious injury closes off an option, such as a hand, the Dash action or easy Concentration. A Lasting injury does both. Keep each scar smaller than its injury: Advantage on one narrow kind of save or check, or a +1 to one roll. Never write an injury that can kill a character or remove them from play.

Crafting and Gathering

The hide of a winter wolf, a flower that opens only under the full moon, a dragon's scale: with this system on, the party can harvest what they defeat and gather what they find, then turn it into potions and magic items. This chapter has the whole system, with the materials and the recipes.

Before You Start

Crafting works best in campaigns with downtime: weeks in a town between adventures, or a stronghold with a workshop. Tell the players which kinds of items you are happy for them to make. A recipe is never a promise that a material exists nearby; you decide what the world holds.

Harvesting Monsters

After a fight, a character can harvest a creature that has a material in the lists below, or one you make up. It takes 10 minutes for a Medium or smaller creature, 30 minutes for a Large one, and 1 hour for a Huge or larger one. The character makes the check that the material names, against the DC for its rarity. On a success, they get one unit of the material. On a failure by 5 or more, the material is ruined.

A material's rarity comes from the creature's Challenge Rating: CR 0 to 2 gives a Common material, CR 3 to 6 Uncommon, CR 7 to 12 Rare, CR 13 to 18 Very Rare, and CR 19 or more Legendary.

Gathering in the Wild

During travel or downtime, a character can spend 1 hour searching a biome for a material that grows or forms there. Make the check against the DC for its rarity. Common and Uncommon materials can be found in most places of the right biome. Rare and stranger materials are found only where you place them: the top of a haunted peak, the heart of an old forest, the bed of a cursed lake.

{{CRAFT_BASE}}

Selling and Buying

A material sells for half its value, like other treasure. Where you allow it, a material can also be bought for its full value, if a merchant has one. Common and Uncommon materials are easy to find in a city; anything rarer is an adventure of its own.

Crafting an Item

To follow a recipe, a character needs:

  • proficiency with the tool that the recipe names;
  • for a permanent magic item, proficiency in the Arcana skill or the ability to cast spells;
  • the materials, the gold, and any base item that the recipe names;
  • a quiet place to work for the recipe's number of workdays (8 hours each).

Very Rare and Legendary recipes need your permission, and most of them need an adventure to find a material.

At the end, the crafter makes a check with the tool against the DC for the item's rarity. On a success, the item is done. On a failure, half the gold and all the time are lost, but the materials can be used again. Two crafters with the right proficiencies can work together; each workday of both counts as two.

Why Recipes Cost What They Cost

A recipe's total cost (its gold plus the value of its materials) is close to the cost of crafting the item in the core rules, shown in the Crafting Cost and Time table, and it takes about the same time. So crafting doesn't make magic items cheap. What it gives is control, and a small saving: a party that harvests its own materials doesn't pay their value. That saves about a quarter of the cost, never more.

Power and Encounters

Crafting makes characters stronger only through the items they make. Count crafted items as part of the treasure you give:

  • When the party crafts an item, give one fewer item of that rarity in the next few treasures.
  • If the party ends up with more magic items than you planned, give each important enemy 10% more Hit Points, or use one more monster of the encounter's lowest CR for each four characters.

If you also use other optional systems, count each system's changes separately.

Making Your Own Recipes

Pick an item from the core rules or from this book, and note its rarity. Choose at least one material of that same rarity, and none rarer. Then add gold until the total cost matches the table (half for a consumable), and use the table's workdays. Tie the material to the item's story: cold boots need a cold beast, a potion of water breathing needs something that lives in the sea.

Gear

The same tools also make ordinary gear: a Potion of Healing, a vial of Antitoxin, a flask of Alchemist's Fire. A gear recipe needs proficiency with its tool, materials and gold worth half of the item's price, and one workday for each 10 GP of the price (minimum 1). No check is needed. The table lists a few; use the same rule for other gear.

Journeys

The road between two adventures can be an adventure too. This system turns long travel into a few quick rolls each day: who leads the way, who watches the skies, how long the food lasts, and what waits behind the next hill. Use it when the trip matters. Skip it when it doesn't.

An optional rule

Journeys are optional. They don't make characters stronger; they make travel cost something. Tell the players the four roles before the trip starts, so that each player can choose one.

The Travel Day

Run a journey one day at a time. Each day has the same four steps:

  1. Weather. Roll a d6 on the biome's Weather table. The effect lasts until the next morning.
  2. Roles. Each character who has a role makes its check (below).
  3. Encounter. Roll a d6. On a 1, roll a d8 on the biome's Encounters table. At a Fast pace, an encounter happens on a 1 or a 2.
  4. Supplies. Roll the supply die (below).

The party travels the usual distance for its pace: 30 miles a day at a Fast pace, 24 at a Normal pace, 18 at a Slow pace. Difficult terrain halves the distance.

The Four Roles

Each character can take one role each day. A role that nobody takes simply fails. A character can take a second role, with Disadvantage on its check. A hireling can take a role; use a +3 bonus for its check.

RoleCheckOn a success
GuideWisdom (Survival) against the biome's Navigation DCThe party keeps its way. On a failure, it gets lost.
ScoutWisdom (Perception) or Dexterity (Stealth), DC 12If an encounter happens, the party sees it first: it can avoid it, or it starts with the other side surprised.
QuartermasterWisdom (Survival) against the biome's Foraging DCThe party finds food and water. Don't roll the supply die today.
LookoutIntelligence (Nature), DC 12You know tomorrow's weather tonight (roll it now), and the camp can't be surprised tonight.

At a Slow pace, the guide and the scout have Advantage. At a Fast pace, they have Disadvantage.

Supplies

The party's food and water is one supply die, from d4 to d12. At the end of each travel day, roll it. On a 1 or a 2, it becomes one size smaller: d12, d10, d8, d6, d4. If a d4 rolls a 1 or a 2, the supplies run out.

Without supplies, the rules for food and water in the Rules Reference of the Player's Compendium apply, and the quartermaster's check becomes the only way to eat.

In a town, each step up the supply die costs 5 GP for each traveler. A mule or a cart lets the party carry a d12; without one, the largest die is a d10.

Getting Lost

When the guide fails, the party is lost. It travels the day's distance, but in a direction that the GM chooses or rolls. The next morning, the guide repeats the check with Advantage. On a success, the guide knows the party went wrong, and roughly where it is. If the guide failed by 5 or more, the party doesn't even know that it is lost until the next check succeeds.

Running Journeys Well

  • Skip days where nothing happens. Roll for the week, then describe it in a few sentences.
  • An encounter isn't always a fight. Many of the entries on the tables are people, signs, and places. Let the players decide what to do with them.
  • Journeys don't make characters stronger, so they don't change how you build encounters. They do drain the party: a party that arrives tired, lost, and hungry is weaker than its level.

Factions and Renown

Guilds, temples, noble houses, secret orders: the world is full of groups that want things, and they notice the heroes who help or hinder them. Renown is a number that tracks how much a faction trusts a character, what it will give, and what it will ask in return.

An optional rule

Factions and renown are optional. They work best in a campaign that stays in one region for a while, where the same groups keep crossing the heroes' path. Tell the players how much renown they have; it is never a secret.

Renown

Each character has a renown score with each faction, from 0 to 50. Everyone starts at 0 with every faction, unless a background says otherwise (a temple acolyte might start at 3 with their temple).

DeedRenown
A small service: deliver a message, guard a door for a night+1
A mission for the faction, done well+2
A great deed that changes the faction's fortunes+5
Work against the faction, or embarrass it−1 to −5
Betray the factionto 0, and enemy

A single deed never gives more than 5 renown. Tell the player when renown changes, and why.

Ranks

Each faction has five ranks. A character reaches a rank when their renown reaches its number: 3, 10, 20, 35, 50. From the third rank on, the faction also asks for a promotion task: a mission that proves the character's loyalty. Until the task is done, the character stays at the rank below.

A character can have renown with many factions, but a faction with a rival watches closely. When a character reaches the third rank in a faction, its rival treats that character as an enemy.

Enemies

A faction that the characters betray, or whose rival they serve too well, becomes an enemy. An enemy faction closes its doors, spreads rumors, and sends agents. Add one of its agents to an encounter now and then, or give it a scene of its own each adventure. To end the enmity, a character must make amends: a costly task that the faction names. Then renown starts again at 0.

Party Renown (Option)

A table can keep one renown score for the whole party with each faction, in place of a score for each character. The ranks, rewards, and promotion tasks stay the same; the party earns and loses renown together.

Rewards

Each faction lists a reward for each rank. Use this ladder for a faction you build yourself:

RenownRankTypical reward
3FriendA warm welcome, a bed, and news. Members answer simple questions.
10MemberA discount of a fifth on the faction's goods and services, and a contact who can arrange meetings.
20TrustedA safe house, a hireling or two, or the loan of an Uncommon magic item for a mission.
35OfficerA small band to command, a spell service of level 5 or lower at no cost, or the gift of a Rare magic item.
50LeaderA seat on the faction's council, a stronghold, or the gift of a Very Rare magic item. The faction's enemies now know your face.

Building a Faction

To make a faction of your own, roll on the Faction Builder in World-Building Tables, or pick from its lists. Then give the faction a goal, a few deeds that earn renown and a few that lose it, a rival, and five ranks from the ladder above. The other faction tables there (Guilds and Trades, Noble Houses, Cults and Secret Orders, Rival Adventurers) give more ideas.

Power and Encounters

Renown doesn't make characters stronger by itself, but its rewards can. Count the magic items that a faction gives or lends as treasure; give less treasure elsewhere. A band of followers that fights beside the characters makes encounters easier: when one does, build the encounter as if the party had one more character for each follower of a CR near the party's level.

Traps & Hazards

A trap is built by someone who wants you hurt. A hazard is the world being the world. Both reward careful players, and both should feel fair when they go off.

Reading an Entry

  • Kind: a trap is made by a creature; a hazard is part of the place.
  • Where: the kind of place where the entry fits best. Move it anywhere it makes sense.
  • Severity: Setback, Dangerous or Deadly, matching the Improvised Damage table in Exploration, Travel & the World. The damage in an entry never goes above that table.
  • Levels, DC and Damage: pick the row for the party's level. The DC is the save DC, and also the DC to find the entry and to stop it, unless the text says otherwise. An entry with no row for the party's level doesn't fit them; pick another.
  • Trigger, Effect, Find it, Stop it: what sets it off, what happens, how a careful character notices it, and how they make it safe.

Running Traps Fairly

  • Leave a clue. A scorched wall, a missing flagstone, a skeleton in an odd place. Players who look for clues should find them.
  • Let a passive check do the work. Compare the DC with each character's Passive Perception before you ask for a roll.
  • Reward a plan. A good idea (a 10-foot pole, a thrown sack, a rope tied to a door) can stop a trap with no roll at all.
  • Give a way out of a repeating trap. When a trap or hazard deals damage round after round, the characters must be able to escape it or stop it within about 3 rounds: an exit, a save or a check that gets a creature out, or a part that breaks. Each repeating entry in this chapter says how.
  • Use Deadly entries rarely. Put them where the story says "this place is guarded", and let the warning signs be loud.

World-Building Tables

Random tables for building a world one piece at a time: settlements, factions, regions, dungeons, rumors, names, and the people who fill them. Roll when you prepare, or roll at the table when the players go somewhere you didn't expect.

Using the Tables

Each table set covers one subject. Roll once on each table in the set and you have the bones of a village, a faction, or a dungeon. You don't have to use every result. Roll, keep what sparks an idea, and roll again or pick when a result doesn't fit.

When two results seem to clash, that is often the best part. A fishing village that is rich in silver, or a holy order that sells poison, asks a question that your players will want answered.

Rolling at the Table

Keep this chapter open behind the screen. When the party asks the name of a town or the owner of a shop, a quick roll is better than a long pause. Write down every result you use, so that the world stays the same the next time the players come back.

Make them yours

Cross out results that don't fit your world and write your own in the margin. A table that you have changed is better than one you haven't.

Pantheons

Two sample pantheons of twelve gods each: the Lantern Court of the cities and fields, and the Old Circle of the wild places. Use them as they are, change them, or replace them with gods of your own.

Using a Pantheon

Each god has a portfolio (what it rules), an alignment, a holy symbol, the cleric domains that suit it, what it wants, and who worships it. The alignment is the god's nature, not a rule for its followers: plenty of good people pray to a neutral god of the sea.

A cleric or a paladin can pick any god. The domains listed for a god are the ones that fit best; "Any" means that none of the domains in this book fits better than another. A god who wants something gives you a story: its priests want it too, and so do its enemies.

Two Pantheons in One World

The two pantheons can live side by side. City folk pray to the Lantern Court, and the hill clans and sea folk keep the Old Circle. Where they meet, some gods are said to be the same god under two names. Decide which ones, if any, and let priests argue about it.

Gods in play

Gods rarely act in person. They speak through omens, dreams, and priests who aren't always right. When a god does act, it should be the biggest moment of the campaign.

Downtime Activities

Things for characters to do between adventures: spread a story, map the hills, throw a feast, find a patron. Each activity costs time and money, and each one can bring a reward or a new adventure.

How Downtime Works

Downtime is measured in workweeks of 5 days. A character does one activity at a time, and pays the activity's cost on top of the usual costs of food and lodging. At the end of the activity, the player makes the check that the activity names and reads the result for the total.

Several characters can do the same activity together. One of them makes the check, and each other character who helps gives that character advantage, unless the activity says otherwise. Each helper pays the cost too.

Complications

Downtime shouldn't always be quiet. Roll on the activity's complications table when the check is a natural 1, or when you want a new story to start. A complication isn't a punishment. It is a hook: a rival, a secret, or a request that leads to the next adventure.

Rewards and balance

The rewards here are small on purpose: a little gold, a contact, Heroic Inspiration, a map. They make a world feel lived in. If an activity would give more than a common magic item's worth of value, cut the reward or add a complication.
