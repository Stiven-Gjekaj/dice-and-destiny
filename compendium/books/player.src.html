1 · Welcome to the Table

Dice & Destiny is a game of collaborative storytelling. You play one hero; the Game Master (GM) plays the world and everyone else in it. There's no winning or losing, just a story you tell together, with dice deciding the uncertain moments.

How Play Works

  1. The GM describes what your character sees and hears.
  2. You say what your character does, in your own words. "I climb the tower." "I ask the innkeeper about the missing girl."
  3. If the outcome is uncertain, the GM asks you to roll.
  4. The GM describes what happens. Repeat.

You can try anything your character could plausibly do. You don't need to find a button for it. Just describe it.

Being a Good Player

  • Describe, don't request. Say "I search the desk for hidden drawers," not "Can I roll Investigation?"
  • Share the spotlight. Ask other players what their characters do.
  • Know your sheet. Learn your own character's abilities. It keeps the game fast.
  • Plan on others' turns so you're ready when it's yours.
  • Play a character, not a calculator. Bad decisions made in character are often the best stories.

Your Dice

The game uses seven dice. "2d6" means roll two six-sided dice and add them. "1d8 + 3" means roll one eight-sided die and add 3.

Dice tips

Keep one complete set of your own, plus an extra d20 for advantage. Different colours help: roll two d20s of different colours and call which one counts before rolling, or use a red d6 for fire damage. Dice that fall off the table are usually rerolled.

2 · The Six Abilities

Every character has six ability scores. Nearly every roll in the game uses one of them.

STRSTRENGTH

Physical power. Melee weapon attacks and damage, Athletics (climbing, jumping, swimming, grappling), carrying capacity. Saves against being pushed, pinned or restrained.

Matters most for: Barbarian, Fighter, Paladin · High: kicks down doors · Low: struggles with a heavy pack

DEXDEXTERITY

Agility, reflexes, precision. Ranged attacks, Finesse weapons, AC in light or no armor, initiative, Acrobatics, Sleight of Hand, Stealth. Saves against explosions and traps.

Matters most for: Rogue, Ranger, Monk, and useful for everyone · High: cat-like · Low: trips over their own feet

CONCONSTITUTION

Health and endurance. Adds to your Hit Points every level. Keeps spells going when you're hit (concentration). Saves against poison, disease and exhaustion. No skills.

Matters for: everyone · High: drinks everyone under the table · Low: winded after stairs

INTINTELLIGENCE

Memory, logic, learning. Arcana, History, Investigation, Nature, Religion. Spellcasting ability for Wizards (and Emberblade Fighters).

Matters most for: Wizard · High: scholar · Low: blunt and practical

WISWISDOM

Awareness, intuition, willpower. Perception (the most-rolled skill), Insight, Medicine, Survival, Animal Handling. Saves against fear, charm and mind control. Spellcasting for Clerics, Druids, Rangers; AC for Monks.

Matters most for: Cleric, Druid, Monk, Ranger · High: sees through everyone · Low: gullible, distracted

CHACHARISMA

Force of personality. Persuasion, Deception, Intimidation, Performance. Spellcasting for Bards, Sorcerers, Warlocks, Paladins. Saves against banishment and possession.

Matters most for: Bard, Paladin, Sorcerer, Warlock · High: commands a room · Low: awkward (presence, not looks)

Proficiency Bonus

When you're trained (proficient) in a skill, weapon, tool or saving throw, add your proficiency bonus. It grows with your total character level:

Levels1–45–89–1213–1617–20
Bonus+2+3+4+5+6

Expertise (Rogues, Bards, Rangers…) doubles your proficiency bonus for that skill.

Advantage & Disadvantage

  • Advantage: roll 2d20, keep the higher.
  • Disadvantage: roll 2d20, keep the lower.
  • They don't stack, and one of each cancels out.
  • Get advantage by: an ally Helping you, attacking a Prone foe in melee, being unseen, clever ideas.

Natural 20 & Natural 1

On an attack roll, a natural 20 always hits and is a Critical Hit (roll damage dice twice); a natural 1 always misses. On checks and saves, a 20 or 1 is just the number, though many GMs make them dramatic.

Heroic Inspiration

The GM awards it for great play. Spend it to reroll any one die. You can only hold one at a time. Humans gain it after every long rest.

4 · Creating a Character

Five steps. Your GM can help, and for a first session a pre-made character is perfectly fine.

1 · Choose a Class

Your class is what you do: fight, cast spells, sneak, heal. It's the biggest choice. See Chapter 12 for all twelve. First-timers: Fighter, Barbarian, Rogue or Cleric.

2 · Choose a Background

Your life before adventuring. Gives ability score increases, two skills, a tool, an Origin feat and starting gear.

3 · Choose a Species

Human, Elf, Dwarf and more. Gives traits like darkvision, resistances, or extra speed. Also choose two languages beyond Common.

4 · Determine Ability Scores

Generate six numbers, assign them, then add your background's increases (+2/+1, or +1/+1/+1).

5 · Fill In the Details

Calculate HP, AC, attacks and skills. Choose an alignment (optional) and give your hero a name, personality, a goal, a bond and a flaw. The roleplay details matter as much as the numbers.

Generating Ability Scores

Standard Array

Assign 15, 14, 13, 12, 10, 8. Fast and fair. Recommended for new players.

Point Buy

27 points. Scores cost: 8 = 0, 9 = 1, 10 = 2, 11 = 3, 12 = 4, 13 = 5, 14 = 7, 15 = 9.

Roll

Roll 4d6, drop the lowest die, six times. Assign as you like. Exciting, but swingy.

Tip: put your highest score in your class's primary ability, the next in Constitution or Dexterity. Use your lowest score to inspire a character flaw.

Alignment

An optional two-word summary of your moral outlook: Lawful / Neutral / Chaotic (order vs. freedom) plus Good / Neutral / Evil (others vs. self). Treat it as a starting point, not a cage.

Personality

Write one line each: a trait (how you act), an ideal (what you believe), a bond (who or what you care about), and a flaw (what trips you up). Give your GM a secret from your past. It may come back.

Make it yours

The mechanics are a frame; the story is yours. Nilah's fire blade is just a longsword with fire damage. Reflavoring the look of any weapon, spell or feature is always allowed if the numbers stay the same.

How to Read It

Ability modifiers are the big numbers. Strength 18 gives +4: that's what Nilah adds to Strength rolls. The small number (18) only matters for a few rules.

Saving throws: Fighters are trained in Strength and Constitution saves, so those add proficiency: Str +4 + 3 = +7.

Skills: Athletics = Str +4 + proficiency +3 = +7. Intimidation uses Charisma: −1 + 3 = +2.

Attack bonus: Str +4 + proficiency +3 = +7 to hit. Damage: weapon die (1d10 two-handed) + Str +4. She reflavors the longsword as fire. Reflavoring is normal and encouraged.

Hit Points: d10 per Fighter level. Level 1: 10 + Con 2 = 12. Levels 2–5: 6 + 2 each = 32. Hardy feat: +5. Total 49.

Spellcasting: Emberblades use Intelligence. DC = 8 + Int 2 + prof 3 = 13. Spell attack = Int 2 + prof 3 = +5.

Sample turn

A corrupted beast charges a child. Nilah moves 30 ft and takes the Attack action: two swings, each d20 + 7 vs. its AC, each hit dealing 1d10 + 4 fire. It's still up, so she uses Action Surge for two more swings. On the beast's turn it rolls 17 against her AC 16, so she uses her reaction to cast Shield (+5 AC), turning it into a miss.

6 · Your Turn in Combat

Combat runs in rounds of about six seconds. Everyone gets one turn per round, in initiative order.

Initiative

When a fight starts, everyone rolls d20 + Dex modifier. Highest goes first; the order stays the same all fight. If you're ambushed, you roll with disadvantage.

Making an Attack

  1. Roll to hit: d20 + attack bonus. Meet or beat the target's AC = hit.
  2. Roll damage: weapon or spell dice + ability modifier.
  3. Crit on a natural 20: roll all damage dice twice, add the modifier once.
  4. Natural 1: always misses.

Attack spells (Fire Bolt) roll to hit like weapons. Saving-throw spells (Burning Hands) make the target roll against your spell save DC instead, often with half damage on a success.

Nilah attacks a wolf (AC 13). She rolls 8 + 7 = 15 → hit. Damage: rolls 6 on the d10, + 4 = 10 fire. With Extra Attack she swings again. If she'd rolled a natural 20: 2d10 + 4.

Opportunity Attacks

If you move out of an enemy's reach, it can use its reaction to attack you once. Avoid it with Disengage.

Cover

Half cover (low wall): +2 AC. Three-quarters (arrow slit): +5 AC. Total: can't be targeted.

Common Conditions

ProneMelee vs. you: advantage. Standing costs half your Speed.
GrappledSpeed 0. Escape with Athletics/Acrobatics.

7 · Hit Points, Healing & Rest

Hit Points

HP is stamina and luck as much as wounds. When damage hits, subtract it from your current HP. Healing can't take you above your maximum. Temporary HP is a buffer lost first; it doesn't stack.

Dropping to 0 HP

You fall Unconscious. At the start of each of your turns, make a death saving throw: a plain d20:

  • 10+ = success. 3 successes = stable.
  • 9 or lower = failure. 3 failures = death.
  • Natural 20: wake up with 1 HP. Natural 1: two failures.
  • Taking damage at 0 HP = a failure (a crit = two).
  • Any healing wakes you up.

If leftover damage after reaching 0 equals your max HP, you die instantly.

Resting

Short Rest · 1 hour

Spend Hit Dice: roll each, add your Con modifier, heal that much. Some features recharge.

Long Rest · 8 hours

Regain all HP, all spent Hit Dice, all spell slots and most features. Lose one level of Exhaustion. Once per 24 hours.

Hit Dice

You have one Hit Die per level. The size depends on class: d6 Sorcerer, Wizard · d8 Bard, Cleric, Druid, Monk, Rogue, Warlock · d10 Fighter, Paladin, Ranger · d12 Barbarian.

Nilah is at 20/49 HP. On a short rest she spends 2 Hit Dice: rolls 7 and 4, +2 each = 15. She's at 35, with 3 Hit Dice left until her next long rest.

Potions of Healing

Healing 2d4+2 · Greater 4d4+4 · Superior 8d4+8 · Supreme 10d4+20. Drinking one is a bonus action.

8 · Spellcasting

Eight classes cast spells. The rules are the same for all of them. Only the spell lists and the ability they use differ.

The Basics

  • Cantrips are level-0 spells you can cast at will, forever. They get stronger at levels 5, 11 and 17.
  • Spell slots are your fuel. Casting a 1st-level spell spends a 1st-level slot. Slots return on a long rest (Warlocks: short rest).
  • Prepared spells: the list of spells you can currently cast. Most casters change it after a long rest.
  • Upcasting: cast a spell with a higher slot for more power (e.g., +1d6 per level).
  • Rituals: some spells can be cast without a slot by taking 10 extra minutes.

Concentration

Many lasting spells need concentration. You can concentrate on only one at a time. When you take damage, make a Con save, DC 10 or half the damage (whichever is higher, max 30) or the spell ends.

Casting Details

  • Casting time: usually an action; some are a bonus action or reaction.
  • Range: self, touch, or a distance.
  • Components: V (speak), S (gesture), M (material; a focus usually covers it, unless it has a gold cost).
  • Duration: instant, a set time, or until dispelled.
  • Attack or save? The spell says. Attack: d20 + spell attack vs. AC. Save: the target rolls vs. your spell save DC.

Burning Hands (1st level): a 15-ft cone of flame. Every creature in it makes a Dex save vs. your DC. Fail: 3d6 fire. Success: half. Cast with a 2nd-level slot: 4d6. It also lights flammable objects nobody is holding.

10 · Leveling Up & Feats

Level-Up Checklist

When your GM says you level up, open your class table (Chapter 12) and:

  1. Hit Points: roll your Hit Die (or take the fixed number) + Con modifier. Add to your max.
  2. Hit Dice: you gain one more.
  3. Proficiency bonus: check if it increased (levels 5, 9, 13, 17).
  4. New features: read every feature listed for your new level.
  5. Level 3: choose your subclass.
  6. Levels 4, 8, 12, 16: Ability Score Improvement or a feat. (Fighters and Rogues get extra.)
  7. Spellcasters: update spell slots, cantrips and prepared spells.
  8. Update numbers that depend on what changed: attack bonus, save DC, skills.
  9. Tell your GM about anything big.

Multiclassing

With GM permission, you can take a level in a second class instead (needs 13 in the primary ability of both classes). Powerful, but complicated. Wait until you know the game.

Feats are special talents that make your character unique.

11 · Glossary

Ability check: d20 + ability modifier (+ proficiency) vs. a DC.

Action: the main thing you do on your turn.

Advantage / Disadvantage: roll 2d20, keep higher / lower.

AC (Armor Class): how hard you are to hit.

Attunement: bonding with a powerful magic item; max three.

Bloodied: at half HP or less.

Bonus action: an extra quick action, only if a feature grants one.

Cantrip: a spell you can cast at will.

Concentration: maintaining one ongoing spell; can break when hit.

Condition: a status effect like Prone or Frightened.

CR (Challenge Rating): how dangerous a monster is.

Critical Hit: a natural 20 on an attack; double damage dice.

d20 Test: any ability check, attack roll or saving throw.

DC (Difficulty Class): the number to meet or beat.

Darkvision: see in darkness as if dim light.

Difficult terrain: each foot of movement costs 2.

28 · Marks

Some heroes carry more than a sword. A Mark is a blessing, a curse or the taint of a Sin that has settled on your character. It gives something, it takes something back, and it changes as your story goes on.

An optional rule

Marks are optional. Use them only if your GM turns them on. They make characters stronger and stories stranger, and your GM's book explains how to keep the fights fair.

What a Mark Is

A Mark is a lasting supernatural change to your character. It isn't a magic item you can drop or a spell that ends at dawn. It lives in you until the story removes it. There are three kinds:

KindWhere it comes fromWhat makes it grow
BlessingA virtue that took root: a saint's touch, a vow kept at great costActing on its virtue
CurseSomething done to you: a hex, a broken taboo, an angry placeThe story (the GM decides)
TaintA Sin that found a way in, usually through power you acceptedUsing its power

You can carry up to three Marks at a time. If the story would give you a fourth, it fails to take hold, and the GM finds another consequence. You can't carry the same Mark twice. If the story would give you a Mark you already carry, its track grows by 1 instead.

Open and Hidden Marks

Every Mark is either open or hidden.

  • Open: you know the Mark and all of its rules, and you keep its track on your character sheet.
  • Hidden: the GM keeps the Mark and its track in secret. You feel its effects (a bonus here, a craving there) but not its name or its numbers.

Blessings and Curses are open unless the GM says otherwise. Taints are hidden, which is why their rules are in the GM's book and not in this one. When a hidden Mark grows, the GM describes a sign that the whole table can see: a flicker in your eyes, a shadow that moves a moment late. A hidden Mark is always revealed to you when it reaches its crisis.

Getting a Mark

A Mark comes to you in one of two ways.

  • From the story. A curse lands on the character who opened the wrong tomb. A saint blesses the one who carried her to safety. You don't choose these; the story does.
  • From a bargain. Your character looks for power and finds someone, or something, willing to give it. The GM sets the price. You always choose whether to accept, but a hidden Mark can hide part of what it costs.

Read the fine print

If a stranger offers you power and won't tell you the price, the price is probably a Taint. That can be a great story. Just know what kind of story you're choosing.

The Parts of a Mark

BenefitWhat the Mark gives you. A Curse can give nothing at all.
CostWhat it takes from you while you carry it.
TrackA number from 0 to 5. A new Mark starts at 0 unless it says otherwise.
GrowsThe trigger that adds 1 to the track.
ThresholdsTrack values where the Mark changes, usually for better and worse at once.
CrisisWhat happens when the track reaches 5.
EndingHow the Mark can end, before and after the crisis.

The Track

When a Mark's trigger happens, its track grows by 1. Unless the Mark says otherwise, a track grows at most once each scene (one fight, or one event of the story), however many times the trigger happens in it. So a Mark can tempt you often, but it can't run from 0 to 5 in a single fight. The track never goes down on its own; only a Mark's own rules, or the end of the Mark, can lower it.

When the track reaches a threshold, the change in the Mark's text applies at once and stays as long as the track is at that value or higher. The track on each Mark has its thresholds in bold, so you can mark it with a pencil as it fills.

The Crisis

When the track reaches 5, the Mark comes to a head. If it was hidden, the GM reveals it to you now. Then you choose one:

Give in

You gain the Mark's full power, which is stronger than anything it gave before. In exchange, at the key moments that the Mark names, the GM steers your character: the GM says what you do in that moment, and you play it. Outside those moments, your character is yours. You can still end the Mark later, but at the higher price the Mark names.

Break free

The Mark ends. It leaves a scar: a small, permanent trace of what you carried, described in the Mark. A scar isn't a Mark and doesn't count toward your three.

Giving in and then breaking free later also leaves the scar. Either way, the choice is yours. A Mark never takes your character away from you unless you choose it.

Ending a Mark

A Mark is part of your story, so it usually ends through the story: a quest, a sacrifice, a choice that proves something. Each Mark says how it ends. Spells such as Remove Curse and Greater Restoration don't end a Mark unless the Mark says they do, though they might be one step of the way. A Mark that ends before its crisis leaves no scar unless its text says so.

An Example of Play

Wren, a halfling rogue, steals a silver coin from a shrine in a drowned village. The GM gives her an open Curse, the kind that makes every lie she tells sound like the truth to her own ears. She writes it on her sheet with its track at 0. Weeks later, Wren lies to the captain of the guard to save a friend, and the lie works a little too well. The GM says the track grows to 1. At 3, the Curse reaches a threshold and grows harsher. Wren's player now knows that the coin must go back to the shrine, at the bottom of the lake, before the track reaches 5.

Meanwhile, Orrin, the party's fighter, took a gift from a smiling stranger without asking the price. Orrin's player doesn't know it, but the GM is tracking a hidden Taint. Each time Orrin uses the gift in a new scene, the GM describes a small sign ("for a heartbeat, your shadow is taller than you are") and quietly adds to a number behind the screen.

29 · Bonds

Heroes don't save the world alone. A Bond is a tie between two characters (friends, rivals, a debt, a sworn oath) that grows stronger through what they live through together, and that can break when one of them betrays it.

An optional rule

Bonds are optional. Use them only if your GM turns them on. They work with or without Marks (Chapter 28).

What a Bond Is

A Bond joins two characters: two player characters, or a player character and an important NPC. Both partners share the same Bond and the same bond die. Each character can have up to two Bonds, and two characters can share only one Bond with each other.

There are four kinds of Bond. Each has a special use, described at the end of this chapter.

KindWhat ties you
FriendTrust, loyalty, and a lot of shared meals
RivalRespect, and the need to be the better one
DebtOne of you owes the other something big
OathA promise you both made, to each other or to a cause

Forming a Bond

A Bond forms after a scene that earns it: one character saves the other's life, two rivals fight to a draw, two strangers swear over a grave. Both players must agree (the GM agrees for an NPC). Choose the kind together, and write the Bond on both character sheets with the partner's name. A new Bond starts with a d4.

Growing the Bond Die

When the two partners share a defining scene (a moment that tests or shows what the Bond means), the GM grows the bond die one step: d4, d6, d8, d10. The d10 is the maximum. A Bond grows at most once each session.

Tamsin and the paladin Hale are Friends with a d4. In a burning chapel, Hale carries Tamsin out on his back while she keeps the smoke away with a spell. The GM says that was a defining scene, and the bond die grows to a d6.

Using a Bond

Once each Short Rest, either partner can spend the Bond to do one of these things. The use is shared: when one partner spends it, neither partner can spend it again until they finish a Short Rest or a Long Rest. Spending the Bond doesn't shrink the die. The partners must be able to see or hear each other.

  • Lend a hand. When your partner makes a d20 Test, roll the bond die and add it to the roll. You can do this after you see the d20, but before the GM says whether the test succeeds.
  • Take the edge off. When your partner takes damage, take a Reaction to reduce the damage by the bond die plus your Proficiency Bonus.
  • Use the special of your Bond's kind.

The GM spends the Bond for an NPC partner. A Bond can't stack with itself: you can't add the same bond die twice to one roll.

Strain

A Bond strains when one partner betrays it: abandons the other in danger, breaks a promise that matters, or does what the kind of Bond forbids (each kind says what strains it). The GM decides, and tells the table. When a Bond strains, its die becomes one size smaller. If a d4 would shrink, the Bond breaks and ends.

Two partners can also end a Bond peacefully, when they both agree. A Bond that ends this way doesn't break.

Bonds and Marks

If your table also uses Marks, the two systems meet in three places:

  • "I'm not letting you become that." When your partner's Taint would grow, you can spend the Bond to stop it. The track doesn't grow this time. If the Taint is hidden, the GM describes a sign that the table can see, and you can spend the Bond without knowing what the sign means.
  • A broken Bond hurts. When a Bond breaks, the betrayed character gains 1 on a Taint that fits the betrayal, such as Wrath or Envy. If the character has no such Taint, the GM can give one.
  • Some Curses block help. A Curse that blocks bond help (it says so) stops your partner from using a bond die, the damage reduction, or a special on you. It never stops your partner from saving you from a Taint.
